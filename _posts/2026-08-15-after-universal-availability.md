When the last serviceable location in the township is passed and lit, it is tempting to declare the work finished. **It isn't. It's barely begun.** Availability is the moment the map stops being about where the wire *isn't* and starts being about what people *do* with it.

To see why, it helps to name the map we are using. The **Digital Opportunities Compass** is a measurement framework, built on more than twenty-five years of research, that guides communities through digital transformation by looking past a single question of access. Rather than asking only whether broadband has reached an address, the Compass organizes the work across *six dimensions*: **Contexts** (the social, economic, and cultural conditions of a place), **Governance** (the policies, leadership, and decision-making that steer the work), **Connectivity** (infrastructure, affordability, and adoption), **Skills** (digital literacy and training), **Applications** (how technology is actually used), and **Outcomes** (the effects on health, learning, income, and civic life). It is, in the plainest terms, a common map that lets local leaders assess where they stand, clarify where they want to go, and align partners around a shared vision.

Seen through that map, universal availability satisfies exactly **one of the six dimensions** — *Connectivity*. The framework was built deliberately so that we would not mistake that single dimension for the whole journey. The research is unambiguous on this point: access, device affordability, and digital skills are the foundation, but **digital opportunity is achieved sustainably only when the entire ecosystem is considered** — not at the moment the fiber reaches the pedestal.

So the next step is to change the question we are asking. We move from *"What should we build?"* to **"What is actually working?"** That is the pivot from *deployment* to *adoption*, and it is where the real capacity-building begins.

Practically, that means turning our attention to the things a buried conduit cannot solve on its own. **Affordability** is usually the first wall people meet — the household that has the wire at the road but cannot carry a monthly subscription is still, functionally, on the wrong side of the divide. Then come **devices**, **digital skills**, and the *human infrastructure of broadband* — the digital navigators, librarians, educators, and trusted neighbors who stand between physical infrastructure and actual use, and without whom the network is only *potential energy.*

This is where I keep returning to Kentaro Toyama and his *Law of Amplification.* **Technology amplifies existing human and institutional capacity; it does not manufacture it.** A township that has universal availability *and* a school with a technology-ready curriculum, entrepreneurs prepared to innovate in the digital economy, and residents comfortable enough to try will watch that fiber compound into real *Outcomes* — health, learning, income, and civic participation. A township with the same fiber and none of that readiness will watch the signal sit idle. The wire is identical in both places. **The amplifier is the community.**

The economic stakes of getting this right are not abstract. Peer-reviewed research on rural communities makes the point plainly: it is *adoption*, not deployment alone, that drives prosperity. Counties that move past availability into high broadband use — above eighty percent — show **business growth rates 213 percent higher, gross domestic product growth 44 percent higher, and per capita income growth 18 percent higher** than comparable counties that remain underused. Just as telling, the places that never close the adoption gap tend to *lose* businesses, jobs, and population. The lesson for any township crossing the availability finish line is that the return on a decade of infrastructure investment is realized only when the network is *utilized and effectively leveraged*, and that the gains accrue most to the residents already there rather than to new arrivals. In other words, adoption is not a soft benefit layered on top of the build. **It is the mechanism through which the build pays for itself.**

And here is the gentle reminder I would fold in: *technology is the T in STEAM* — Science, Technology, Engineering, Arts, and Mathematics. The infrastructure is the enabling letter, not the whole word. The point was never the network. It was the *aspiration* the network makes reachable — the person who can now start the business, reach the clinic, or finish the degree.

So, in my words, the next step after universal availability is this: **stop measuring wires and start measuring lives.** Build the *Governance* and local capacity to sustain what was deployed. Invest in the human infrastructure. Close the affordability and skills gaps. And put in place a way to *keep learning* — the Compass to see where you are, and something like the **Digital Opportunities Intelligence Network (DOIN)**, our policy learning machine powered by Project Compass, to tell you not only *what happened* but *why*, and *what to do next.*

**Availability is the starting line we spent a decade racing toward. Adoption, and the opportunity it unlocks, is the race.**

### Sources of Inspiration

This essay draws on frameworks and research that continue to shape the Compass series:

- **The Digital Opportunities Compass** — the six-dimension framework that anchors this work. Rhinesmith, C., Dagg, P. R., Bauer, J. M., Byrum, G., & Schill, A. (2023). *Digital Opportunities Compass: Metrics to Monitor, Evaluate, and Guide Broadband and Digital Equity Policy.* Working paper. Ann Arbor, MI: Merit Network, Inc., and East Lansing, MI: Quello Center, Michigan State University.

- **The academic foundation behind the Compass** — for the pairing of a practitioner tool with a statistical "policy learning" approach. Bauer, J. M., Dagg, P. R., Rhinesmith, C., Byrum, G., & Schill, A. (2023). *A Comprehensive Framework to Monitor, Evaluate, and Guide Broadband and Digital Equity Policy.* Presented at TPRC51. Available at SSRN: https://ssrn.com/abstract=4557340

- **The human infrastructure of broadband** — for the recognition that digital navigators, educators, librarians, and trusted community members are essential to turning access into use. Rhinesmith, C., & Prasad, R. (2025). *From the Ground Up: Investing in the Human Infrastructure of Broadband*, with contributions from C. Stratton and L. Clark. Wilmette, IL: Benton Institute for Broadband & Society.

- **Sustaining the human infrastructure of broadband** — for the framing of where this work has been and where it is headed. Bryne, A. (2025, January). *The Human Infrastructure of Broadband: Looking Back, Looking Around, and Looking Ahead.* Wilmette, IL: Benton Institute for Broadband & Society.

- **The economics of adoption** — for the evidence that broadband *use*, not deployment alone, drives income, entrepreneurship, and business investment in rural places. Weinstein, A., Erouart, M., & Dewbury, A. (2024). *Beyond Connectivity: The Role of Broadband in Rural Economic Growth and Resilience.* Hartland, VT: Center on Rural Innovation (CORI).

- **The Law of Amplification** — for the principle of technology as an *amplifier* of existing human and institutional capacity rather than a substitute for it. Toyama, K. (2015). *Geek Heresy: Rescuing Social Change from the Cult of Technology.* New York: PublicAffairs.

- **The Power of Yet** — for the growth-mindset framing of community capacity as a condition that can change over time. Dweck, C. S. (2006). *Mindset: The New Psychology of Success.* New York: Random House.

- **The Digital Opportunities Intelligence Network (DOIN)** — my own concept for a continuous community measurement and policy-learning system that moves us from *what happened* to *why*, and *what to do next*. DOIN builds directly on the Digital Opportunities Compass and the broader body of digital-opportunity research cited above, extending that foundation into a decision-support and policy-learning framework. Kronemeyer, J. F. (2026). *Digital Opportunities Intelligence Network (DOIN).* EUPConnect Collaborative, inspired by Project Compass (Merit Network) and Bauer et al. (2023).
