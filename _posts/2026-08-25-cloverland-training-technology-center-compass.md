# A School Reimagined: The Cloverland Training & Technology Center Inches Closer to Reality

For years, many of us in Michigan's Eastern Upper Peninsula have talked about a challenge that extends well beyond broadband, energy, education, or economic development. We simply do not have enough local pathways to train, attract, and retain the workforce needed to support the infrastructure that modern communities depend upon.

The shortage is visible everywhere.

Utilities need lineworkers and substation technicians. Broadband providers need fiber splicers and network technicians. Businesses need skilled information technology professionals. Communities need electricians, low-voltage installers, cybersecurity specialists, and emerging energy technicians.

The question has never been whether the need exists.

The question has been how we create the institutional capacity to address it.

That is why recent developments surrounding the former Washington Elementary School campus in Sault Ste. Marie are worth paying attention to.

## From Closed School to Regional Workforce Asset

In June 2026, Cloverland Electric Cooperative announced that it had reached an agreement with Sault Area Public Schools regarding the Washington Elementary property. According to public reporting, the project entered a due-diligence and zoning review process, with site preparation potentially beginning after approvals are completed and major construction activity anticipated during future building seasons.

At first glance, the project could be mistaken for a simple building reuse effort.

It is much more than that.

Cloverland has publicly described a vision for a Training & Technology Center that would support workforce training, safety education, apprenticeship opportunities, and industry-recognized certifications related to utility operations, energy systems, and broadband infrastructure.

Rather than allowing a former school building to sit vacant, the proposal would preserve its educational purpose while adapting that mission for a new generation of learners.

There is something appropriate about that.

Schools are fundamentally places where communities invest in human development. The students may change. The curriculum may change. The skills required by society may change. But the purpose remains remarkably consistent.

## An Idea Whose Time Has Come

One of the more encouraging aspects of the proposal is the broad coalition of support that has emerged around it.

Public statements from Cloverland note support from organizations including Lake Superior State University, Sault Area Public Schools, the Sault Ste. Marie Tribe of Chippewa Indians, labor organizations, utility industry partners, and regional elected officials.

That level of alignment is significant.

Large community projects rarely succeed because of a single organization acting alone. They succeed when multiple institutions recognize a shared challenge and choose to solve it together.

Over the last several years, Michigan's Upper Peninsula has made substantial investments in physical infrastructure. Fiber networks have expanded. Grid modernization continues. School facilities are being reimagined. Communities are exploring resilient energy systems and emerging technologies.

Yet infrastructure alone is not enough.

Broadband requires technicians.

Power systems require trained workers.

Smart buildings require installers and operators.

Modern communities require people.

The Cloverland Training & Technology Center starts from that simple understanding.

## The Human Infrastructure Behind Economic Development

Too often, discussions about economic development focus on buildings, equipment, and funding.

Those things matter.

But research consistently shows that the long-term strength of a region is closely linked to the strength of its institutions and its people.

The most successful communities invest not only in physical infrastructure but also in what might be called human infrastructure: education systems, workforce pipelines, collaborative networks, and opportunities for lifelong learning.

Viewed through that lens, the proposed Washington campus redevelopment becomes something larger than a construction project.

It becomes an investment in regional self-reliance.

Instead of importing talent whenever a skilled position opens, the region gains the ability to develop more of that talent locally.

Instead of watching young people leave in search of technical careers, it creates pathways that allow them to build those careers closer to home.

Instead of relying exclusively on external expertise, it strengthens local capacity.

Those benefits extend well beyond Cloverland itself.

## Why Workforce Development Matters for Broadband

Readers of this blog know that I often write about digital opportunity, broadband, and community capacity.

One lesson has become increasingly clear.

Broadband deployment is not simply a technology challenge.

It is a workforce challenge.

Fiber networks require construction crews, technicians, designers, inspectors, and operators. Community institutions require cybersecurity professionals, network engineers, and technology coordinators. Emerging digital infrastructure increasingly intersects with energy systems, operational technology, and smart building platforms.

The boundaries between infrastructure sectors continue to blur.

The workforce needed to support them must evolve as well.

Public descriptions of the Cloverland center suggest training opportunities that could span utility operations, broadband infrastructure, communications technology, and related skilled trades.

That kind of convergence reflects where the future is heading.

## A Community Asset in the Making

There are still steps ahead.

Funding strategies continue to evolve. Regulatory processes must be completed. Design and construction work remain ahead. Like any ambitious project, the center's final form will depend on many decisions that are still being made.

But the trajectory is increasingly clear.

What was once a conceptual discussion has progressed to property agreements, public information sessions, community engagement, legislative conversations, and active planning.

That matters.

Communities often spend years talking about what they need.

Occasionally they reach a moment when those conversations begin turning into reality.

The proposed Cloverland Training & Technology Center appears to be approaching that moment.

## Looking Beyond the Building

The most important outcome may not be the building itself.

It may be what that building represents.

A community choosing to reinvest in education.

Institutions choosing to collaborate rather than compete.

A region recognizing that workforce development is infrastructure.

And a shared belief that the future of the Upper Peninsula will depend not only on the systems we build, but on the people we prepare to operate, maintain, and improve them.

If the project continues moving forward, the former Washington Elementary School campus may once again become a place where futures are built.

Only this time, those futures may include lineworkers, broadband technicians, energy specialists, cybersecurity professionals, and the next generation of infrastructure leaders who will help power and connect the Upper Peninsula for decades to come.

That is not simply the redevelopment of a school.

It is the redevelopment of community capacity.
