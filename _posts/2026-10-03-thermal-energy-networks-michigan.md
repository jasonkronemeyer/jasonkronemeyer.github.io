Michigan has an opportunity to modernize how communities heat and cool buildings without repeating the same infrastructure mistakes of the past. Thermal Energy Networks (TENs) are shared underground systems that move thermal energy between multiple buildings, allowing them to exchange heat and cooling efficiently across a district or neighborhood. In practical terms, TENs can reduce the need for each building to maintain separate boiler, chiller, and rooftop HVAC systems while improving long-term operating efficiency.

This matters in Michigan because the state has a large inventory of schools, municipal buildings, multifamily housing, and commercial developments that could benefit from more resilient and lower-maintenance thermal systems. TENs are not a futuristic concept reserved for niche demonstration projects. They are a scalable infrastructure tool that can be tailored to community needs, building types, and utility planning.

## What Thermal Energy Networks are

Thermal Energy Networks connect multiple buildings to a common loop of underground piping. Instead of every building producing or rejecting heat on its own, the system allows nearby structures to share thermal resources. In a district-scale design, buildings can move excess heat from one location to another or use the ground and shared loop as a thermal buffer.

The systems can support:

- Shared heating and cooling across multiple buildings
- Lower peak demand on electric systems
- Reduced dependence on individual building mechanical equipment
- Integration with new construction and retrofit projects

TENs can be designed to work with a mix of building types, from schools and municipal campuses to housing developments and commercial centers.

## Why Michigan should pay attention

### 1. Infrastructure modernization

Michigan's public institutions and local governments face constant pressure to maintain aging building systems while balancing capital budgets. TENs can help by reducing the number of standalone mechanical systems that require ongoing maintenance and replacement. They also fit naturally with broader infrastructure planning, including utility trenching, road projects, and campus redevelopment.

For schools, municipal buildings, and housing clusters, the value is straightforward: fewer mechanical assets to maintain, more predictable lifecycle costs, and the ability to align thermal infrastructure with long-term development plans.

### 2. Economic efficiency

TENs can lower long-term operating costs by reducing mechanical redundancy and improving system efficiency. Buildings connected to a shared loop can avoid duplicative heating and cooling equipment. That can reduce both capital costs and ongoing utility expenses, especially when multiple users share an infrastructure base.

For developers and community planners, this creates a stronger project case. A district-scale system can spread fixed costs across multiple users, making it more attractive to finance and easier to manage over time. It also reduces the risk of systems becoming stranded as building use changes or technology standards evolve.

### 3. Workforce development

TENs require skilled labor in drilling, piping, controls, commissioning, and maintenance. That creates opportunities for skilled trades, apprenticeships, and community college programs. In Michigan, where workforce development is increasingly tied to infrastructure investment, this could become part of a broader strategy to support year-round construction and operations employment.

The workforce implications are especially relevant for regions looking to build practical, locally rooted technical careers without depending on a single industry or a narrow labor market.

### 4. Energy resilience

TENs reduce exposure to fuel supply volatility and localized building-level equipment failure. Because the system is shared and distributed, it can improve reliability for participants while reducing dependence on individual mechanical systems that may be costly or difficult to replace.

This is particularly important for critical public buildings such as schools, public safety facilities, and civic campuses, where reliable thermal service matters during emergencies and periods of grid stress.

### 5. Public health and safety

A district thermal system can reduce the need for combustion-based heating systems inside buildings. That can improve indoor air quality and reduce the noise, maintenance burden, and fire risk associated with rooftop units and fuel storage. For schools and housing, those benefits matter directly.

TENs also support more predictable, centralized management of equipment and performance. That can make building operations safer and simpler over the life of the facility.

## Policy steps Michigan can take

To move from concept to action, Michigan should consider a practical policy package that aligns thermal infrastructure with broader public priorities:

- Allow TENs to be treated as an eligible utility infrastructure option for pilot programs and cost recovery structures.
- Support demonstration projects in schools, municipal campuses, multifamily housing, and commercial districts.
- Integrate TEN planning into infrastructure coordination alongside broadband, transportation, and water investments.
- Provide workforce development funding and partnerships with community colleges, unions, and training providers.
- Create local planning guidance so communities understand how TENs fit into zoning, development review, and capital planning.

This is not a one-size-fits-all approach. The right model will vary by geography, building stock, and utility structure. But the underlying opportunity is clear: TENs can help Michigan build infrastructure that is more efficient, more resilient, and easier to maintain.

## Conclusion

Thermal Energy Networks offer Michigan a practical way to modernize infrastructure while lowering long-term costs and improving community resilience. They are especially relevant for places with clustered buildings, aging mechanical systems, and pressure to deliver reliable public services.

As communities weigh the next generation of infrastructure investment, TENs deserve serious attention. They combine efficiency, flexibility, and long-term value in a way that fits the realities of public planning and local economic development.

Michigan does not need to chase the newest technology trend to benefit from this approach. It needs to identify efficient infrastructure tools that improve service reliability, reduce operating strain, and support smarter public investment. Thermal Energy Networks fit that test.
