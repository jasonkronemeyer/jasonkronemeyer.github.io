When I led the formation of the EUPConnect Collaborative, I learned an uncomfortable lesson about economic development: sometimes the hardest part of building something new is convincing people that what you are building belongs in the conversation at all.

I came to the work from K-12 education. That background shaped how I saw broadband, technology, workforce development, and community capacity. It also created a problem. In economic-development circles, I was not necessarily perceived as an economic developer. I was an education person talking about broadband, a technology person talking about community development, or someone from the schools trying to participate in conversations that traditionally belonged to municipalities, economic-development organizations, telecommunications providers, utilities, and business leaders. It was difficult to gain traction.

In hindsight, however, that experience revealed something much more important than a question of professional identity. It exposed a weakness in the way we define economic development itself. We are very good at recognizing an investment once it looks like economic development. A new employer arrives. A factory expands. A grant is awarded. A building goes up. Jobs are announced. Capital investment is counted. Those are important outcomes, but what about everything that had to exist before those outcomes became possible?

What is the economic value of a student developing technical skills that will not become economically visible for another five years? What is the value of a school district building infrastructure that can eventually become a workforce training environment? What is the value of connecting a library, a township, a college, an internet provider, and a community organization before anyone knows exactly which project those relationships will produce? Most importantly for a rural region like the Eastern Upper Peninsula, what is the value of making the next opportunity possible?

I did not have good language for all of that when we started EUPConnect. I do now.

## We Have Been Measuring the Wrong Kind of Return

Traditional return on investment asks an important question: if I invest something, what do I get back? The problem is not ROI. The problem is treating one kind of return as though it represents the whole system.

I have come to think of infrastructure and community investment through four dimensions: **Owner Return, User Return, Community Return, and Opportunity Value**. I call this **Holistic ROI**. The framework asks us to consider not simply how much value an investment creates, but who receives that value, who bears its costs, what capabilities remain afterward, and what new possibilities those capabilities create.

I recently applied this framework to AI and data-center infrastructure, where the distinctions become particularly visible. A project can produce excellent financial returns for its owner while transferring infrastructure costs to other people. Conversely, infrastructure can be designed so that an investment creates capabilities extending far beyond the original project. I increasingly believe this framework explains something we were doing through EUPConnect long before I had a name for it.

## Owner Return: Does It Work for the Organization Making the Investment?

Every sustainable collaboration has to begin here. An internet service provider cannot build infrastructure indefinitely without a viable business case. A school district cannot invest resources without advancing its educational mission. A municipality has fiduciary responsibilities to its residents. A nonprofit has a mission and funding constraints. An employer has operational requirements. Collaboration does not mean pretending those interests do not exist.

The better question is whether we can design projects in which organizational self-interest and regional interest reinforce one another. That is Owner Return. Economic development often begins here because it is the easiest return to quantify. Capital investment, operating costs, revenue, utilization, and financial sustainability can fit into a spreadsheet, but they represent only one dimension of value.

## User Return: Does Someone's Life Actually Get Better?

Broadband taught many rural communities the importance of this distinction. We can count miles of fiber. We can count locations passed. We can measure advertised speeds. None of those measurements, by themselves, tell us whether a family can afford service, whether a student can reliably complete schoolwork, whether a small business can use cloud applications, or whether a community institution can perform its mission more effectively.

Infrastructure is not successful simply because it exists. It has to create useful capability for someone. User Return asks whether people and organizations receive better service, capability, reliability, affordability, or access.

That perspective came naturally from education. Educators constantly encounter the difference between providing a resource and producing an outcome. Putting a computer in front of a student does not create learning. Installing Wi-Fi does not create digital literacy. Buying equipment does not create technical competence. Perhaps my K-12 background was not a weakness in approaching economic development after all. Perhaps it helped me see a part of the equation that conventional economic-development metrics sometimes miss.

## Community Return: What Are We Still Holding When the Grant Is Gone?

This may be the most important test for rural development. Grants end. Programs end. Vendors leave. Consultants finish their contracts. What remains?

Within Holistic ROI, Community Return is the portion of an investment's value that becomes durable community capacity. For the Eastern Upper Peninsula, that can mean fiber infrastructure that can be used by others. It can mean people who have earned industry-recognized credentials. It can mean stronger relationships among schools, municipalities, libraries, tribal communities, businesses, nonprofits, and higher education. It can mean better data, local technical expertise, institutional knowledge, grant-writing capability, or organizations that now know how to work together.

This suggests a much harder question for every publicly supported project: **Five years after the funding ends, what will our community possess that it did not possess before?** If the answer is only a final report, we probably have not created much Community Return. If the answer is infrastructure, skills, institutions, relationships, data, and increased local ability to act, we have created something much more durable.

## Opportunity Value: What Can We Do Now That We Could Not Do Before?

The fourth dimension is the one I wish I had been able to articulate when EUPConnect began. I call it **Opportunity Value**. It measures the possibilities created by an investment even when we cannot yet identify the project that will ultimately use them.

A fiber route built exclusively for one facility has value. A fiber route designed so that schools, businesses, public institutions, future developments, or additional providers can eventually use its capacity has something more. It has optionality.

The same principle applies to human infrastructure. Train someone for one narrowly defined task and we have solved an immediate workforce problem. Give that person a foundation of transferable technical skills, recognized credentials, professional relationships, and opportunities for continued learning, and we have created the capacity to respond to opportunities we do not know about yet.

That is the difference between infrastructure as an input and infrastructure as a platform. It is also why Holistic ROI treats spare capacity, interoperability, institutional capability, resilience, and future development options as sources of value. It is why a regional broadband map, a shared data resource, a training partnership, or a relationship among institutions can be economically important before it produces a conventional transaction.

## Maybe the Education Person Belonged in the Room After All

Early in the formation of EUPConnect, my K-12 background sometimes made it harder to establish credibility within traditional economic-development circles. Today, I see that differently. Education was not adjacent to the economic-development work. **Education was part of the economic infrastructure.**

So were broadband, libraries, workforce programs, community organizations, local governments, colleges, utilities, technical training, data, and the relationships connecting all of them. Economic development does not begin when a business announces an investment. It begins years earlier, when a community develops the capacity to say **yes** when an opportunity finally arrives.

Does the investment work for the organization making it? That is **Owner Return**. Does it improve the experience and capability of the people who use it? That is **User Return**. Does some durable portion of that value stay in the community? That is **Community Return**. Does what we built expand the range of things our region can do next? That is **Opportunity Value**.

Put together, those questions offer a different way of understanding the work of the EUPConnect Collaborative:

> **A good EUPConnect project works for the organization investing in it, improves life for the people using it, leaves durable capacity in the region, and expands what the Eastern Upper Peninsula can do next.**

That last part matters enormously. Rural communities cannot predict every economic opportunity that will emerge over the next decade. Artificial intelligence, advanced manufacturing, energy systems, broadband, connected infrastructure, agriculture, healthcare, and technologies we have not encountered yet will continue changing the economic landscape. We do not need to predict all of them. We need communities capable of responding to them.

That may be the return on investment we have been overlooking. **The greatest economic-development asset a rural community can build is not any single project. It is the capacity to pursue the opportunity that comes after it.**
