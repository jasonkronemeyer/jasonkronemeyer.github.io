## Executive Summary

Communities typically evaluate infrastructure through conventional return-on-investment measures: capital cost, operating expense, revenue, payback period, and financial risk. These measures are essential, but they are incomplete when an asset enables broad downstream activity. Infrastructure creates value not only for its owner, but also by expanding what users can do, affecting the surrounding community, and preserving possibilities that may not be foreseeable when an investment is made.

Brett M. Frischmann (2012), in *Infrastructure: The Social Value of Shared Resources*, provides an economic foundation for this argument, shifting attention from the supply of infrastructure to what people can produce when shared resources are available.

I propose Holistic ROI as a decision-making lens—not an established accounting standard—that considers **Owner Return, User Return, Community Return, and Opportunity Value**. It is a conceptual proposal, not a validated scoring method. Its purpose is to broaden analysis without weakening financial discipline: identify who may benefit, compare realistic alternatives, and distinguish evidence from uncertainty. The spreadsheet is not wrong, but for infrastructure, it is often incomplete.

## Key Takeaways

**Infrastructure is an input, not the outcome.** Roads, broadband networks, schools, energy systems, and other shared resources enable activities by other people. Their value may show up in what students, businesses, patients, or other users are able to do.

**Owner Return remains necessary, but it is not total return.** Infrastructure still has to be financed, operated, maintained, and renewed. Holistic ROI does not replace those responsibilities. It prevents the owner's income statement from being mistaken for a complete account of the value produced by the asset.

**Trace User Return to capability and outcomes.** The value of connectivity is not the connection by itself, just as the value of electricity is not limited to the delivery of kilowatt-hours. Ask what users can do with the infrastructure, whether they can access and afford it, and what outcomes are supported by evidence.

**Treat Community Return as something to investigate, not assume.** Infrastructure may affect employment, business formation, public safety, health access, and other community outcomes. Identify who gains or bears costs, the pathway from the investment to the outcome, and what might have happened without it.

**Treat Opportunity Value as options, not projected revenue.** Infrastructure may preserve room for experimentation, adaptation, and resilience, but uncertain future uses are not guaranteed benefits. Use scenarios to describe possibilities rather than assigning them false precision.

**Include access and governance in the analysis.** Ownership alone does not determine whether infrastructure enables new value. The rules governing who can use an asset, for what purposes, at what price, and with how much freedom to innovate also matter.

## The Limits of Conventional ROI

When communities debate infrastructure, the conversation eventually finds its way to return on investment. The question is reasonable: if we are going to spend millions of dollars building fiber, modernizing a school, extending utilities, constructing a community facility, or upgrading an electrical system, we should be able to explain what we receive in return. The problem is not ROI itself, but how narrowly we tend to define the return.

Traditional financial analysis is very good at measuring what happens inside a transaction. How much will the infrastructure cost? What revenue will it generate? How many customers will pay for it? How long is the payback period? What is the internal rate of return?

Those questions matter, but infrastructure produces effects far beyond the entity that owns it and the customers who directly purchase its services. A broadband connection becomes a classroom, a telehealth connection, a business platform, an agricultural tool, a public-safety resource, and perhaps something that has not yet been invented. A school is not merely a building. A transportation network is not valuable only because of tolls or fares. An electrical system does more than sell kilowatt-hours. In each case, infrastructure becomes an input into other people's ability to create value.

That distinction is central to Brett M. Frischmann's (2012) *Infrastructure: The Social Value of Shared Resources*. Published by Oxford University Press, the book argues that understanding infrastructure requires looking beyond conventional supply-side economics to the demand side: what people are able to produce when infrastructure resources are available to them.

That demand-side insight provides an economic foundation for the framework I call **Holistic ROI**. The four categories that follow are my synthesis; they should not be read as a framework proposed by Frischmann. I define Holistic ROI through four complementary forms of return—**Owner Return, User Return, Community Return, and Opportunity Value**. These are not substitutes for financial analysis; they are an attempt to finish the analysis that traditional ROI begins.

## Infrastructure Is Valuable Because Other Things Depend on It

One of Frischmann's (2012) important contributions is to define infrastructure functionally. What matters is not simply whether we call something infrastructure, but what role the resource plays in enabling other productive activities.

Infrastructure resources are inputs used to produce a wide range of downstream goods and services. Some of those outputs are commercial. Others are public, social, civic, cultural, scientific, or educational. The book therefore shifts attention toward infrastructure's demand side and asks how people's use of shared resources generates value throughout society.

Consider a rural fiber network. A conventional business case might measure construction cost, operating expense, subscriber penetration, and monthly revenue. Those numbers tell us something important—whether the network can sustain itself economically—but they do not tell us everything the network produces.

A student taking an online college course uses it. So does a home-based entrepreneur, a small manufacturer transferring engineering files, someone working remotely for an employer hundreds of miles away, a patient connecting with a specialist, or a local government employee accessing a cloud application. None of those outcomes necessarily appears as additional revenue on the network operator's income statement, yet without the network some of them may not occur. The infrastructure becomes an input into other people's ability to create value.

This is the distinction between the **return to the infrastructure** and the **return enabled by the infrastructure**—both of which Holistic ROI attempts to consider. Infrastructure can make an activity possible without guaranteeing that it will occur; affordability, adoption, skills, complementary services, and governance can all affect whether that potential becomes an outcome.

## Owner Return

The first category is the familiar one: **Owner Return asks whether the infrastructure creates sufficient value for the entity responsible for building, financing, and maintaining it.** Revenue matters, as do lifecycle cost, maintenance requirements, avoided operating expense, asset utilization, resilience, depreciation, financing cost, and long-term sustainability.

This part of Holistic ROI should not be diminished simply because other forms of value exist: infrastructure that cannot be maintained is not made sustainable by having large theoretical social benefits. Owner Return is only one lens, however, and the crucial mistake comes when we treat it as though it represents the entire return.

Frischmann's (2012) analysis helps explain why that assumption can fail. Infrastructure can generate substantial social gains that are diffuse, downstream, or difficult to incorporate into ordinary market transactions. Consequently, the financial return captured by the owner need not equal the total value generated by the resource.

For a private investment, Owner Return may appropriately dominate the decision. For public and community infrastructure, stopping there can create a serious measurement problem.

## User Return

The second category asks a different question: **What becomes possible for the people and organizations using the infrastructure?** This is where the demand-side perspective becomes particularly useful.

A broadband subscriber's return is not simply the number of megabits received for a monthly payment. The useful return comes from what those megabits enable.

A farmer may gain access to precision-agriculture tools. A student can participate in education that was previously geographically inaccessible. A business can reach a national market. A household may avoid hours of travel through telehealth. A worker can participate in an employment market far larger than the local labor market. These are possible pathways, not guaranteed effects; a careful analysis asks which capabilities users actually gain and what evidence links them to the infrastructure.

The infrastructure is an input, and the user's activity produces the outcome. That distinction matters because conventional ROI can easily assign most of the measurable value to the owner of an asset while overlooking the productive capacity created for its users; in Holistic ROI, that productive capacity counts.

## Community Return

The third category gets harder to measure, but perhaps more important: **Community Return captures benefits and costs that extend beyond the owner and individual user.** Economists generally describe many such effects as externalities or spillovers. Infrastructure can create value for people who are not parties to the original transaction, and Frischmann (2012) argues that these effects are particularly significant when infrastructure supports the production of public and social goods.

Imagine that broadband enables someone to launch a business from a rural community. The entrepreneur receives User Return and the network provider receives Owner Return, but the effects do not necessarily stop there. The business might hire employees who spend money locally; a vacant building may be occupied, local tax revenue may increase, or a young family may be able to remain in the community rather than relocate for employment. These are plausible spillover pathways, not automatic returns to credit to the network. The effects may be difficult to attribute to the original infrastructure investment, but that does not make them imaginary.

The same reasoning can apply to educational attainment, public safety, health access, workforce participation, entrepreneurship, civic participation, housing development, and countless other systems. Infrastructure intersects with each without completely owning the outcome, which is where infrastructure economics becomes community-development economics. A business case should therefore identify the pathway, the other factors involved, who receives the benefit or cost, and what would likely happen without the investment. Holistic ROI therefore asks us to look deliberately for those distributed returns instead of treating them as incidental side effects.

## Opportunity Value

The fourth category may be the most important and the hardest to place on a spreadsheet: **Opportunity Value is the value of preserving the ability to do things we cannot yet specify.** Here Holistic ROI intersects particularly strongly with Frischmann's (2012) discussion of option value.

Frischmann (2012) challenges the assumption that infrastructure should always be optimized around foreseeable, immediately appropriable uses. Infrastructure can support many downstream activities, and users themselves possess knowledge about their needs and opportunities that infrastructure owners cannot possibly possess. Preserving access can therefore preserve possibilities for experimentation and future value creation.

Think about the Internet. It would have been extraordinarily difficult for someone financing communications infrastructure decades ago to identify every future application that would eventually travel across it. Search engines, streaming video, cloud computing, telemedicine, smartphones, remote work, connected vehicles, artificial intelligence, and thousands of other applications emerged because general-purpose infrastructure created room for experimentation. The inability to forecast those outcomes was not evidence that their value was zero; it was evidence that the future was unknowable.

That suggests an important distinction in infrastructure planning: **Unused capacity and unvalued capacity are not necessarily the same thing.** Some capacity may be excessive, but some represents optionality: room for growth, experimentation, resilience, and uses that have not yet emerged. This makes Opportunity Value different from simply predicting future financial ROI. It asks instead: **What possibilities does this investment preserve or create?** Its inverse may be even more revealing: **What future options disappear if we do not build it?**

## From Spillover-Rich Infrastructure to Generative Infrastructure

In his Quello Center article ["The Internet as 'Spillover-Rich' Infrastructure"](https://quello.msu.edu/the-internet-as-spillover-rich-infrastructure/), Mitchell Shapiro (2016) applies Frischmann's reasoning directly to the Internet. The article describes the Internet's broad, difficult-to-predict public and social benefits and the risk that optimizing for foreseeable private returns can constrain other socially valuable uses.

I think there is a useful word for infrastructure exhibiting this quality: **generative**. Generative infrastructure does not merely deliver a predefined service. It expands the capacity of other people to create.

A road permits destinations its builder did not select. Electricity powers inventions the utility never designed. Broadband carries applications the network operator did not develop. A community laboratory enables experiments its funder never imagined. A school's digital infrastructure supports learning experiences that did not exist when the building was constructed. These examples show why infrastructure cannot always be evaluated like a conventional product: its greatest outcome may be somebody else's.

## Commons Does Not Simply Mean Free

There is another important implication in Frischmann's (2012) work. He connects infrastructure with **commons management**: a resource-management principle, not a synonym for free access or public ownership. In this account, access is nondiscriminatory with respect to users' identity or intended use, and it can be implemented through public or private institutions (Shapiro, 2016). The relevant question is how rules governing access affect what users can subsequently produce.

A privately owned network can have generative characteristics, while a publicly owned asset can be poorly utilized. A cooperative can create broad participation or become restrictive. Ownership alone does not determine the result, so we need to ask about architecture, governance, and access alongside ownership.

Who can use the infrastructure? For what purposes? At what price? Who gets excluded? Can users innovate without first obtaining permission? Does the system preserve capacity for new entrants and unforeseen applications? Together, those questions help determine whether an infrastructure investment simply extracts value from existing demand or generates conditions from which new value can emerge.

## A Better Infrastructure Business Case

None of this means communities should approve projects because advocates can imagine possible benefits; Holistic ROI should make infrastructure analysis **more rigorous**, not less. A useful business case should explicitly evaluate all four dimensions. Before estimating returns, it should state the decision being considered, identify realistic alternatives (including the status quo), set a time horizon, and identify affected groups. The framework is a way to organize evidence, not a formula for adding unlike benefits into a single score.

An existing tool offers one way to make parts of this analysis more concrete. The Digital Opportunities Compass organizes broadband and digital-equity assessment around context, governance, connectivity, skills, applications, and outcomes, with indicators intended to establish baselines and track change over time (Rhinesmith et al., 2023). It could inform measures of User and Community Return in broadband projects, though it is not itself a comprehensive infrastructure ROI framework.

* **Owner Return** examines capital cost, operating cost, financing requirements, direct revenue, avoided costs, lifecycle requirements, and financial risks.
* **User Return** tracks who can access and afford the infrastructure, whether it is adopted, what capabilities it enables, and what outcomes are supported by evidence.
* **Community Return** identifies positive and negative effects beyond direct users and owners, who receives them, the plausible pathway from infrastructure to effect, and how the outcome compares with the baseline.
* **Opportunity Value** describes future choices, capabilities, resilience, and experimentation the asset may enable. Use scenarios to show uncertainty and identify options that could be foreclosed; do not treat every imaginable future use as a realized benefit.

The analytical discipline comes from keeping these categories separate. A speculative community benefit should not be disguised as owner revenue. An owner's financial loss should not disappear merely because social benefits exist. A difficult-to-quantify future opportunity should not automatically be converted into a fictional dollar amount to make a benefit-cost ratio work.

For example, in an illustrative rural-fiber proposal, the owner analysis might examine construction and lifecycle costs; the user analysis might track affordability, adoption, and documented changes in what subscribers can do; and the community analysis might test whether local employment or business activity changed relative to a credible baseline. Future capacity or resilience could be described through scenarios rather than counted as certain revenue. This example shows questions to investigate, not evidence that these outcomes will occur.

For each claimed return, record the indicator, data source, time period, affected group, comparison baseline, and uncertainty. The same outcome may matter from more than one perspective, but it should not be counted twice in any combined estimate. Some returns can be monetized, some can be measured without monetization, and others may need to be described as uncertainties and options. **A holistic framework does not require pretending everything has a precise price. It requires refusing to pretend that everything without a price has no value.**

## Opportunities for Further Research

For Holistic ROI to become more than a useful way to frame discussion, it needs to be tested in practice. Researchers could apply the categories to completed and proposed infrastructure projects and ask which indicators distinguish owner, user, and community returns without double-counting. They could also examine what baselines and comparison groups help identify infrastructure's contribution, how results differ across project types and governance arrangements, and whose benefits or costs are missed by conventional financial analysis.

Opportunity Value raises a further challenge: how can researchers assess options that were available when a decision was made without judging the investment only in hindsight? Longitudinal and comparative studies could document which anticipated capabilities materialize, which do not, and what unforeseen uses emerge, while keeping uncertain future benefits distinct from realized outcomes. This work could show whether the four-part framework adds decision-relevant information and how it can be used without turning uncertain possibilities into guaranteed returns.

## The Spreadsheet Wasn't Wrong. It Was Incomplete.

This is ultimately the problem Holistic ROI is intended to address. Conventional ROI remains an essential tool: communities need to know whether infrastructure is affordable, maintainable, and fiscally responsible. But when we apply an owner-centered financial tool to infrastructure whose purpose is to enable thousands of decentralized activities, we risk measuring only the part of the system that is easiest to see.

Frischmann's (2012) demand-side theory gives us a different starting point. Infrastructure matters precisely because other things depend upon it, and the social value generated through those downstream activities can exceed what an infrastructure owner can capture through ordinary transactions.

Holistic ROI carries that insight into practical community decision-making: it asks us to account for the owner without stopping at the owner, the user without stopping at consumption, and returns distributed across the community, while recognizing the value of possibilities we cannot yet predict. The most consequential return on an infrastructure investment may not be what the infrastructure produces today; it may be **what someone else becomes able to produce tomorrow.**
