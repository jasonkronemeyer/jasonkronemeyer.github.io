## Executive Summary

Communities typically evaluate infrastructure through conventional return-on-investment measures: capital cost, operating expense, revenue, payback period, and financial risk. These measures are essential, but they are incomplete when the asset under consideration enables broad downstream activity. Infrastructure does not create value only for its owner. It also expands what users can do, generates benefits and costs across the surrounding community, and preserves possibilities that may not be foreseeable when the original investment decision is made.

Brett M. Frischmann's *Infrastructure: The Social Value of Shared Resources* provides an economic foundation for understanding this wider field of value. Frischmann shifts attention from the supply of infrastructure to the demand side: the many productive, civic, educational, scientific, cultural, and social activities that become possible when people can use shared resources. His work explains why the financial return captured by an infrastructure owner may represent only a fraction of the value generated through the asset's downstream uses.

Holistic ROI translates that insight into a practical four-part framework: **Owner Return, User Return, Community Return, and Opportunity Value**. Owner Return measures the asset's direct financial and operational sustainability. User Return measures the capabilities and outcomes created for people and organizations that use it. Community Return captures spillovers extending beyond the immediate transaction. Opportunity Value recognizes the future choices, experiments, resilience, and productive possibilities preserved by the investment.

This framework does not weaken financial discipline or assign speculative dollar amounts to every possible benefit. It makes infrastructure analysis more complete by separating distinct forms of return, identifying who receives them, and acknowledging uncertainty without treating everything that cannot be priced as valueless. The central argument is straightforward: the spreadsheet is not wrong, but for infrastructure, it is often incomplete.

## Key Takeaways

**Infrastructure should be evaluated by what it enables, not only by what it earns.** Roads, broadband networks, schools, energy systems, laboratories, and other shared resources become inputs into activities performed by other people. Their most consequential outcome may appear in a student's education, a business's growth, a patient's access to care, or a future application that the infrastructure owner did not create.

**Owner Return remains necessary, but it is not total return.** Infrastructure still has to be financed, operated, maintained, and renewed. Holistic ROI does not replace those responsibilities. It prevents the owner's income statement from being mistaken for a complete account of the value produced by the asset.

**User Return focuses on capability.** The value of connectivity is not the connection by itself, just as the value of electricity is not limited to the delivery of kilowatt-hours. The meaningful return comes from the education, work, production, communication, care, and participation that users can achieve through the infrastructure.

**Community Return makes spillovers visible.** Infrastructure can influence employment, business formation, public safety, educational attainment, health access, tax capacity, civic participation, and population retention. These effects may be distributed among people who are not parties to the original transaction, but they remain part of the infrastructure's social and economic significance.

**Opportunity Value is more than projected future revenue.** It represents the value of preserving the ability to pursue possibilities that cannot yet be specified. General-purpose, accessible infrastructure creates room for experimentation, adaptation, resilience, and innovation. Uncertainty is therefore not always a defect in an infrastructure business case. Sometimes it is where a substantial share of the value resides.

**Holistic analysis should not force every return into a dollar amount.** Some effects can be monetized, others can be measured with nonfinancial indicators, and still others should be described as options, uncertainties, or strategic capabilities. Analytical rigor comes from distinguishing these forms of evidence rather than manufacturing false precision.

**Architecture, access, and governance help determine whether infrastructure becomes generative.** Ownership matters, but ownership alone does not determine the result. The rules governing who may use an asset, for what purposes, at what price, and with how much freedom to innovate shape whether the infrastructure extracts value from existing demand or enables new value to emerge.

## The Limits of Conventional ROI

When communities debate infrastructure, the conversation eventually finds its way to return on investment.

The question is reasonable. If we are going to spend millions of dollars building fiber, modernizing a school, extending utilities, constructing a community facility, or upgrading an electrical system, we should be able to explain what we receive in return.

The problem is not ROI itself. The problem is how narrowly we tend to define the return.

Traditional financial analysis is very good at measuring what happens inside a transaction. How much will the infrastructure cost? What revenue will it generate? How many customers will pay for it? How long is the payback period? What is the internal rate of return?

Those questions matter. But infrastructure produces effects far beyond the entity that owns it and the customers who directly purchase its services. A broadband connection becomes a classroom, a telehealth connection, a business platform, an agricultural tool, a public-safety resource, and perhaps something that has not yet been invented. A school is not merely a building. A transportation network is not valuable only because of tolls or fares. An electrical system does more than sell kilowatt-hours.

The infrastructure becomes an input into other people's ability to create value.

That distinction is central to Brett M. Frischmann's *Infrastructure: The Social Value of Shared Resources*. Published by Oxford University Press, the book argues that understanding infrastructure requires looking beyond conventional supply-side economics to the demand side: what people are able to produce when infrastructure resources are available to them.

That insight provides an important economic foundation for what I call **Holistic ROI**.

I define Holistic ROI through four complementary forms of return:

**Owner Return + User Return + Community Return + Opportunity Value**

These are not substitutes for financial analysis. They are an attempt to finish the analysis that traditional ROI begins.

## Infrastructure Is Valuable Because Other Things Depend on It

One of Frischmann's important contributions is to define infrastructure functionally. What matters is not simply whether we call something infrastructure, but what role the resource plays in enabling other productive activities.

Infrastructure resources are inputs used to produce a wide range of downstream goods and services. Some of those outputs are commercial. Others are public, social, civic, cultural, scientific, or educational. The book therefore shifts attention toward infrastructure's demand side and asks how people's use of shared resources generates value throughout society.

Consider a rural fiber network.

A conventional business case might measure construction cost, operating expense, subscriber penetration, and monthly revenue. Those numbers tell us something important: whether the network can sustain itself economically.

But they do not tell us everything the network produces.

A student taking an online college course uses it. So does a home-based entrepreneur. So does a small manufacturer transferring engineering files. So does someone working remotely for an employer hundreds of miles away. So does a patient connecting with a specialist. So does a local government employee accessing a cloud application.

None of those outcomes necessarily appears as additional revenue on the network operator's income statement. Yet without the network, some of them may not occur.

This is the distinction between the **return to the infrastructure** and the **return enabled by the infrastructure**.

Holistic ROI attempts to measure both.

## Owner Return

The first category is the familiar one.

**Owner Return asks whether the infrastructure creates sufficient value for the entity responsible for building, financing, and maintaining it.**

Revenue matters. So do lifecycle cost, maintenance requirements, avoided operating expense, asset utilization, resilience, depreciation, financing cost, and long-term sustainability.

This part of Holistic ROI should not be diminished simply because other forms of value exist. Infrastructure that cannot be maintained is not made sustainable by having large theoretical social benefits.

But Owner Return is only one lens.

The crucial mistake comes when we treat it as though it represents the entire return.

Frischmann's framework helps explain why that assumption can fail. Infrastructure can generate substantial social gains that are diffuse, downstream, or difficult to incorporate into ordinary market transactions. Consequently, the financial return captured by the owner need not equal the total value generated by the resource.

For a private investment, Owner Return may appropriately dominate the decision. For public and community infrastructure, stopping there can create a serious measurement problem.

## User Return

The second category asks a different question:

**What becomes possible for the people and organizations using the infrastructure?**

This is where the demand-side perspective becomes particularly useful.

A broadband subscriber's return is not simply the number of megabits received for a monthly payment. The useful return comes from what those megabits enable.

A farmer may gain access to precision-agriculture tools. A student can participate in education that was previously geographically inaccessible. A business can reach a national market. A household may avoid hours of travel through telehealth. A worker can participate in an employment market far larger than the local labor market.

The infrastructure is an input. The user's activity produces the outcome.

That distinction matters because conventional ROI can easily assign most of the measurable value to the owner of an asset while overlooking the productive capacity created for its users.

In Holistic ROI, that productive capacity counts.

## Community Return

The third category gets harder to measure, but perhaps more important.

**Community Return captures benefits and costs that extend beyond the owner and individual user.**

Economists generally describe many such effects as externalities or spillovers. Infrastructure can create value for people who are not parties to the original transaction, and Frischmann argues that these effects are particularly significant when infrastructure supports the production of public and social goods.

Imagine that broadband enables someone to launch a business from a rural community.

The entrepreneur receives User Return. The network provider receives Owner Return. But the effects do not necessarily stop there.

The business might hire employees. Those employees spend money locally. A vacant building may be occupied. Local tax revenue may increase. A young family may be able to remain in the community rather than relocating for employment.

Those secondary effects are not easily credited to the original infrastructure investment. But that does not make them imaginary.

This is where infrastructure economics becomes community-development economics.

The same reasoning can apply to educational attainment, public safety, health access, workforce participation, entrepreneurship, civic participation, housing development, and countless other systems. Infrastructure intersects with each without completely owning the outcome.

Holistic ROI therefore asks us to look deliberately for those distributed returns instead of treating them as incidental side effects.

## Opportunity Value

The fourth category may be the most important and the hardest to place on a spreadsheet.

**Opportunity Value is the value of preserving the ability to do things we cannot yet specify.**

Here Holistic ROI intersects particularly strongly with Frischmann's discussion of option value.

His analysis challenges the assumption that infrastructure should always be optimized around foreseeable, immediately appropriable uses. Infrastructure can support many downstream activities, and users themselves possess knowledge about their needs and opportunities that infrastructure owners cannot possibly possess. Preserving access therefore preserves possibilities for experimentation and future value creation.

Think about the Internet.

It would have been extraordinarily difficult for someone financing communications infrastructure decades ago to identify every future application that would eventually travel across it. Search engines, streaming video, cloud computing, telemedicine, smartphones, remote work, connected vehicles, artificial intelligence, and thousands of other applications emerged because general-purpose infrastructure created room for experimentation.

The inability to forecast those outcomes was not evidence that their value was zero. It was evidence that the future was unknowable.

That suggests an important distinction in infrastructure planning:

**Unused capacity and unvalued capacity are not necessarily the same thing.**

Some capacity may be excessive. But some represents optionality: room for growth, experimentation, resilience, and uses that have not yet emerged.

This makes Opportunity Value different from simply predicting future financial ROI.

It asks instead: **What possibilities does this investment preserve or create?**

Its inverse may be even more revealing: **What future options disappear if we do not build it?**

## From Spillover-Rich Infrastructure to Generative Infrastructure

In his Quello Center article ["The Internet as 'Spillover-Rich' Infrastructure"](https://quello.msu.edu/the-internet-as-spillover-rich-infrastructure/), Mitchell Shapiro applies Frischmann's reasoning directly to the Internet. The argument centers on the Internet's ability to support an extraordinarily broad and difficult-to-predict collection of public and social goods. The article also highlights the danger of optimizing infrastructure around uses that generate foreseeable private returns while foreclosing activities that could generate greater social returns.

I think there is a useful word for infrastructure exhibiting this quality:

**Generative.**

Generative infrastructure does not merely deliver a predefined service. It expands the capacity of other people to create.

A road permits destinations its builder did not select. Electricity powers inventions the utility never designed. Broadband carries applications the network operator did not develop. A community laboratory enables experiments its funder never imagined. A school's digital infrastructure supports learning experiences that did not exist when the building was constructed.

This is why infrastructure cannot always be evaluated like a conventional product.

Its greatest outcome may be somebody else's outcome.

## Commons Does Not Simply Mean Free

There is another important implication in Frischmann's work.

He connects infrastructure with **commons management**, meaning arrangements through which communities receive access to resources under broadly nondiscriminatory terms. The relevant question is not simply public ownership versus private ownership. It is how rules governing access affect what users can subsequently produce.

That distinction matters enormously for community infrastructure.

A privately owned network can have generative characteristics. A publicly owned asset can be poorly utilized. A cooperative can create broad participation or become restrictive. Ownership alone does not determine the result.

We therefore need to ask about architecture, governance, and access alongside ownership.

Who can use the infrastructure? For what purposes? At what price? Who gets excluded? Can users innovate without first obtaining permission? Does the system preserve capacity for new entrants and unforeseen applications?

Those questions help determine whether an infrastructure investment simply extracts value from existing demand or generates conditions from which new value can emerge.

## A Better Infrastructure Business Case

None of this means communities should approve projects because advocates can imagine possible benefits.

Holistic ROI should make infrastructure analysis **more rigorous**, not less.

A useful business case should explicitly evaluate all four dimensions.

**Owner Return** examines capital cost, operating cost, financing requirements, direct revenue, avoided costs, lifecycle requirements, and financial risks.

**User Return** identifies the new capabilities users receive and the measurable economic or social benefits resulting from those capabilities.

**Community Return** examines the positive and negative spillovers outside the direct transaction, who receives those effects, and whether they can reasonably be attributed to the infrastructure.

**Opportunity Value** considers the future choices, capabilities, resilience, and experimentation the asset makes possible, as well as the options that could be foreclosed by failing to invest or by designing the infrastructure too narrowly.

The analytical discipline comes from keeping these categories separate.

A speculative community benefit should not be disguised as owner revenue. An owner's financial loss should not disappear merely because social benefits exist. A difficult-to-quantify future opportunity should not automatically be converted into a fictional dollar amount to make a benefit-cost ratio work.

Some returns can be monetized. Some can be measured without monetization. Others may need to be described as uncertainties and options.

**A holistic framework does not require pretending everything has a precise price. It requires refusing to pretend that everything without a price has no value.**

## The Spreadsheet Wasn't Wrong. It Was Incomplete.

This is ultimately the problem Holistic ROI is intended to address.

Conventional ROI remains an essential tool. Communities need to know whether infrastructure is affordable, maintainable, and fiscally responsible.

But when we apply an owner-centered financial tool to infrastructure whose purpose is to enable thousands of decentralized activities, we risk measuring only the part of the system that is easiest to see.

Frischmann's demand-side theory gives us a different starting point. Infrastructure matters precisely because other things depend upon it, and the social value generated through those downstream activities can exceed what an infrastructure owner can capture through ordinary transactions.

Holistic ROI carries that insight into practical community decision-making.

It asks us to account for the owner without stopping at the owner. To account for the user without stopping at consumption. To look for returns distributed across the community. And, perhaps most importantly, to recognize the value of possibilities we cannot yet predict.

Because the most consequential return on an infrastructure investment may not be what the infrastructure produces today.

It may be **what someone else becomes able to produce tomorrow.**
