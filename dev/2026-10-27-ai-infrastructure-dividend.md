Beyond Public vs. Private: Why the AI Economy Needs a Cooperative DividendBy Jason F. KronemeyerThe conversation about artificial intelligence infrastructure is increasingly framed as a debate between public ownership and private investment. On one side are advocates for municipally owned computing resources and public-interest AI. On the other are the hyperscale data centers and infrastructure funds investing hundreds of billions of dollars into the AI economy.That framing may be too narrow.A better question is this: If AI infrastructure becomes as essential to society as electricity, telecommunications, transportation, and broadband, how should communities participate in the value it creates?The answer may not be found entirely in government ownership or private markets alone. Instead, it may lie in a model America has used successfully for nearly a century: cooperatives.Learning from Rural ElectrificationMany rural communities were bypassed during the early expansion of electric utilities because serving sparsely populated regions generated limited returns for private investors. The response was not to nationalize the electric system. Nor was it to simply accept uneven access.Instead, communities organized.Rural electric cooperatives emerged as a way for local residents, businesses, and institutions to collectively own critical infrastructure. Federal financing accelerated deployment, but local ownership ensured that the benefits remained rooted in the communities being served.The result helped transform rural America.Today, much of the AI infrastructure conversation resembles those earlier debates around electricity, telephone service, and broadband. Large investors are building the foundations of a new economic era, but the benefits are not naturally distributed. Communities often experience the impacts first: increased energy demand, land use pressures, water consumption, workforce challenges, and infrastructure costs.The question is whether communities can also become participants in the value chain.The Concept of an AI Infrastructure DividendThe underlying assumption driving data-center investment is that AI will become foundational infrastructure.If investors are correct, then AI compute may soon occupy a role similar to electric generation, transportation networks, and telecommunications. It becomes a productive asset upon which entire industries depend.Historically, society has often accepted private ownership of foundational infrastructure when paired with obligations that produce public benefits:Universal telephone serviceRural electrificationEducational broadcastingUtility service territoriesBroadband deployment requirementsAI infrastructure may require a similar social compact.One possibility is an AI Infrastructure Dividend.Under this model, a small portion of the value generated by compute infrastructure would be permanently dedicated to public purposes:Workforce developmentCommunity collegesPublic researchCivic innovationPublic-interest AI applicationsEmergency managementCommunity technology initiativesThe allocation would not be charitable. It would become part of the infrastructure itself, creating a durable public benefit alongside private returns.Why Cooperatives MatterThis is where cooperatives become particularly interesting.Cooperatives already operate significant infrastructure throughout North America. Electric cooperatives manage distribution networks. Telecommunications cooperatives provide broadband service. Agricultural cooperatives coordinate production and logistics. Credit unions offer community-owned financial services.These organizations possess something often missing from AI infrastructure discussions: established governance models that align long-term community interests with infrastructure investment.Imagine several possible cooperative approaches.Cooperative Compute UtilitiesElectric cooperatives could operate community-scale computing facilities alongside existing energy assets.Many rural electric cooperatives already manage:SubstationsFiber networksRights-of-wayCommunications systemsEnterprise IT operationsTraining facilitiesAs distributed AI applications become more common, cooperatives may find opportunities to deploy regional computing resources much like they deployed broadband networks over the last decade.The goal would not be competing with hyperscale facilities operated by Microsoft, Google, Amazon, or other cloud providers.Instead, the goal would be providing local, trusted, and mission-specific infrastructure.Community Ownership ModelsAnother possibility is direct community investment.Rather than relying exclusively on outside capital, communities, anchor institutions, schools, hospitals, utilities, and cooperatives could jointly invest in regional AI infrastructure.Ownership could create several benefits:Revenue sharingLocal governanceWorkforce development commitmentsCommunity computing allocationsEducational accessResearch partnershipsIn this model, communities become stakeholders rather than hosts.Cooperative Data CommonsData may ultimately prove as important as compute.Rural regions often possess valuable data assets related to agriculture, forestry, transportation, healthcare, energy systems, natural resources, and community development.Today much of this data is fragmented across organizations.Cooperatives could create trusted governance mechanisms that allow members to contribute data while retaining appropriate local control.The result could be regional data commons that support public-interest research, workforce development, economic planning, and AI applications without concentrating control in a handful of organizations.A Unique Opportunity for Rural AmericaFor regions like Michigan's Upper Peninsula, the opportunity is unlikely to come from attracting hyperscale campuses.The more realistic path may be participation.Electric cooperatives, municipal utilities, educational institutions, tribal nations, community foundations, and regional development organizations already possess many of the ingredients necessary to create community benefit from the AI economy.The Upper Peninsula has a long history of solving problems through collaboration. Its broadband initiatives, workforce partnerships, public-private collaborations, and cooperative utilities demonstrate a pattern of regional innovation that often emerges because no single organization can succeed alone.That tradition may become increasingly important.Communities that organize early may be able to secure a share of the economic value generated by AI infrastructure. Communities that wait may find themselves purchasing access indefinitely from systems they do not influence.The Next Generation of Cooperative InfrastructureThe debate should not be framed as public versus private data centers.Private investment will remain essential. The scale of capital required to build modern AI infrastructure guarantees that.The more important discussion is how society creates mechanisms that allow private investment, public institutions, and community organizations to generate shared value.Cooperatives offer one proven framework.They represent a middle path between government ownership and investor ownership. They align incentives across generations. They prioritize stewardship over extraction. Most importantly, they create pathways for communities to participate in economic transformation rather than simply react to it.The history of electricity, telephony, and broadband suggests that every foundational infrastructure eventually requires institutions capable of balancing efficiency, innovation, and public benefit.The AI economy may be approaching that moment now.The question is not whether communities should participate.
