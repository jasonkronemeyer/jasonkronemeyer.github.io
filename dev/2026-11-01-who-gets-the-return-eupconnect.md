# Who Gets the Return?

## A Holistic ROI Framework for Infrastructure and Regional Economic Development

## Executive Summary

Return on investment is one of the most familiar concepts in economic decision-making, yet conventional ROI becomes incomplete when applied to enabling infrastructure such as broadband. The problem is not necessarily that infrastructure produces an insufficient return. The problem is that much of its return occurs **outside the balance sheet of the organization making the investment**. A broadband provider may capture subscriber revenue. A household may capture value through remote employment, education, healthcare, reduced travel, or entrepreneurship. A school may gain instructional capacity. A healthcare organization may improve service delivery. A business may become more productive. Local governments and emergency services may operate more effectively. Those activities can, in turn, contribute to employment, income, business growth, workforce development, institutional capacity, resilience, and future investment. No single balance sheet contains the whole return.

This paper proposes **Holistic Return on Investment**, or **Holistic ROI**, as a framework for understanding infrastructure value through four complementary categories:

1. **Owner Return:** What financial and operational value does the owner or operator capture?
2. **User Return:** What value do people and organizations capture or create by using the infrastructure?
3. **Community Return:** What value accrues beyond individual transactions to the surrounding community or regional economy?
4. **Opportunity Value:** What new choices, capabilities, investments, relationships, and productive activities become possible because the investment exists?

The framework can be expressed simply as:

> **Holistic ROI = Owner Return + User Return + Community Return + Opportunity Value**

This is a conceptual accounting framework rather than a claim that all four forms of return can or should always be converted into dollars and mathematically summed. The central thesis is straightforward:

> **The full economic value of infrastructure is not limited to the financial return captured by its owner. It also includes the value users create through its use, the value accruing to the surrounding community, and the Opportunity Value created by expanding what people and institutions can do next.**

The research of Mitchell Shapiro provides an important intellectual foundation for this argument. His work on broadband as "spillover-rich" infrastructure, generative versus extractive ownership, community broadband, rural electric cooperatives, public fiber investment, and Michigan's broadband expansion ecosystem consistently examines how communications infrastructure can create economic and social value beyond what its owner can directly monetize. ([Shapiro 2016a](https://quello.msu.edu/the-internet-as-spillover-rich-infrastructure/); [Shapiro 2016c](https://quello.msu.edu/community-broadband-as-generative-infrastructure/); [Shapiro 2016d](https://quello.msu.edu/btop-middle-mile-fiber-the-potential-of-generative-special-access/); [Shapiro 2019](https://reicenter.org/upload/documents/colearning/Electric%20Cooperatives%20and%20the%20Digital%20Divide.pdf); [Shapiro 2020](https://reicenter.org/upload/documents/MShapiro_2020Fellowship_FinalReport.pdf))

The EUPConnect Collaborative provides a regional setting in which to apply and test that proposition. EUPConnect's experience suggests that infrastructure investment can produce returns not only for infrastructure owners, but also for users, institutions, communities, and subsequent investments whose feasibility is improved by what has already been built.

## The Problem With Traditional Infrastructure ROI

Traditional ROI assumes a relatively clear relationship between an investor, an investment, and the financial return captured by that investor. A business purchases equipment because the resulting productivity or revenue is expected to exceed its cost. A telecommunications provider builds a network because subscriber revenue is expected to justify construction, financing, maintenance, and operating expenses. That logic is necessary. Infrastructure owners must be economically sustainable. But that logic becomes incomplete when applied to enabling infrastructure. The reason is simple:

> **The return can escape the boundary of the organization making the investment.**

A broadband provider can build fiber and capture subscription revenue. The household using that fiber may subsequently capture value through employment, education, healthcare, entrepreneurship, communication, entertainment, or avoided travel. A school may capture educational value. A healthcare provider may improve patient care. A manufacturer may increase productivity. A community organization may deliver services more efficiently. A new business may become feasible because reliable high-capacity connectivity exists. Economic value has been created even though much of it never appears as revenue to the infrastructure owner. The distinction becomes particularly important in rural regions. When an infrastructure project's costs are concentrated in one organization while its benefits are distributed across many organizations and users, a productive investment can appear economically unattractive when evaluated from the perspective of only one balance sheet. This suggests a different question. Instead of asking only:

> **Will this infrastructure generate enough return for its owner?**

regional economic development should also ask:

> **What economic activity becomes possible because this infrastructure exists, who captures that value, and what additional opportunities become possible as a result?**

## Infrastructure Creates Value Beyond the Owner

The need for a wider conception of infrastructure return is not entirely new. Mitchell Shapiro's research on telecommunications and community broadband provides an important foundation for understanding why conventional financial return captures only part of the value created by enabling infrastructure. In [*The Internet as “Spillover-Rich” Infrastructure*](https://quello.msu.edu/the-internet-as-spillover-rich-infrastructure/), Shapiro explicitly draws upon Brett Frischmann's conception of infrastructure as a resource supporting broad social value that may be difficult to predict, quantify, and internally monetize. Shapiro applies that reasoning to telecommunications policy, emphasizing that communications infrastructure can support benefits extending well beyond the transactions through which the network operator earns revenue. ([Shapiro 2016a](https://quello.msu.edu/the-internet-as-spillover-rich-infrastructure/))

The distinction matters economically. If infrastructure enables productive activity whose value appears elsewhere, the financial return captured by the owner is not equivalent to the total economic value produced by the infrastructure. That observation becomes more consequential when Shapiro turns from infrastructure economics to ownership. In [*Extractive vs. Generative Ownership of Telecom Infrastructure*](https://quello.msu.edu/extractive-vs-generative-ownership-it-matters/) and [*Community Broadband As Generative Infrastructure*](https://quello.msu.edu/community-broadband-as-generative-infrastructure/), Shapiro applies Marjorie Kelly's distinction between generative and extractive ownership to telecommunications. In doing so, he considers how ownership, governance, competitive conditions, and organizational purpose influence whether the broader value-producing capabilities of telecommunications infrastructure are cultivated or constrained. ([Shapiro 2016b](https://quello.msu.edu/extractive-vs-generative-ownership-it-matters/); [Shapiro 2016c](https://quello.msu.edu/community-broadband-as-generative-infrastructure/))

Importantly, Shapiro does not treat every community-owned network as automatically successful. He discusses differences in governance, finance, institutional context, and management, and closes by identifying questions about community-network performance that require less polarized empirical research. ([Shapiro 2016c](https://quello.msu.edu/community-broadband-as-generative-infrastructure/)) The larger proposition is more useful:

> **Infrastructure ownership influences how value is captured, but infrastructure can produce additional value that appears beyond the owner.**

That provides an important starting point for Holistic ROI.

---

## Public Investment and the Value That Appears Elsewhere

Shapiro's analysis of publicly supported fiber extends this reasoning. In [*Exploring the Value of Public Investment in “Generative” Fiber Infrastructure*](https://quello.msu.edu/btop-middle-mile-fiber-the-potential-of-generative-special-access/), he examines the Broadband Technology Opportunities Program's Comprehensive Community Infrastructure initiative. Citing the program evaluation available at the time, he reports more than 42,000 miles of new fiber and 24,000 miles of upgraded fiber, while framing the investment within larger questions about competition, infrastructure economics, nondiscriminatory access, and social value. ([Shapiro 2016d](https://quello.msu.edu/btop-middle-mile-fiber-the-potential-of-generative-special-access/)) The economic importance of this perspective is that the value of public infrastructure cannot necessarily be inferred solely from the revenue received by whoever operates the physical asset. Infrastructure can enable activity. That activity can generate value. And the entity creating or capturing that value does not have to be the entity owning the infrastructure. This establishes an important distinction for Holistic ROI between:

**value captured by the infrastructure owner** and **value enabled by the infrastructure investment.**

## From Community Infrastructure to Rural Economic Development

Shapiro's later work brings this reasoning directly into rural economic development. His 2019 [*Electric Cooperatives and the Digital Divide: Helping Connect Rural Americans to 21st Century Opportunity*](https://reicenter.org/upload/documents/colearning/Electric%20Cooperatives%20and%20the%20Digital%20Divide.pdf) examines rural electric cooperatives deploying or considering high-performance fiber networks. The report develops information and recommendations for expanding broadband **and the benefits it can bring** to underserved rural communities. ([Shapiro 2019](https://reicenter.org/upload/documents/colearning/Electric%20Cooperatives%20and%20the%20Digital%20Divide.pdf)) That distinction is important. The objective is not merely broadband deployment. It is broadband deployment **and the benefits produced through broadband**.

Shapiro's study is particularly relevant to the Eastern Upper Peninsula because its Michigan analysis includes a dedicated discussion of Cloverland Electric Cooperative. ([Shapiro 2019](https://reicenter.org/upload/documents/colearning/Electric%20Cooperatives%20and%20the%20Digital%20Divide.pdf), “Cloverland Electric Cooperative” section) By 2020, the institutional boundary of Shapiro's research had expanded further. His [*Aligning Resources to Connect Rural Michigan to Pandemic-Ready Broadband Networks*](https://reicenter.org/upload/documents/MShapiro_2020Fellowship_FinalReport.pdf) examines broadband availability across Michigan's planning regions, rural electric cooperatives, shortcomings in broadband availability data, public subsidies, regional planning organizations, state policy, and innovative business and partnership models. One section is explicitly titled **"Strengthening Michigan's Broadband Expansion Ecosystem."** ([Shapiro 2020](https://reicenter.org/upload/documents/MShapiro_2020Fellowship_FinalReport.pdf))

The report describes collaboration among rural electric cooperatives, regional planning organizations, local governments, community anchor institutions, and other stakeholders as part of that ecosystem-building work. ([Shapiro 2020](https://reicenter.org/upload/documents/MShapiro_2020Fellowship_FinalReport.pdf)) The unit of analysis has therefore expanded. The broadband problem is no longer simply:

> Who should build the network?

It increasingly becomes:

> How can institutions, resources, data, public programs, partnerships, and infrastructure be aligned to extend broadband and its benefits?

This is the point at which Shapiro's research intersects especially strongly with the EUPConnect experience.

# From Spillover Value to Holistic ROI

Shapiro's work helps establish why infrastructure value cannot be inferred solely from the financial performance of its owner. Holistic ROI extends that proposition by asking **where the resulting value appears** and **what additional possibilities the investment creates**. The provenance boundary matters: Shapiro does not propose the four-category framework or use “Opportunity Value” in the specific sense defined here. **Owner Return, User Return, Community Return, and Opportunity Value are this paper's analytical synthesis.** Shapiro's work helps explain the problem the framework is designed to address. The framework therefore distinguishes four categories of return.

## 1. Owner Return

**Owner Return** is the financial and operational value captured by the organization owning or operating the infrastructure. Depending on the asset, measures might include:

- revenue;
- operating margin;
- utilization;
- operating savings;
- maintenance savings;
- reliability;
- customer retention;
- avoided costs;
- asset appreciation;
- lifecycle cost;
- and financial sustainability.

Owner Return matters because infrastructure that cannot be operated and maintained sustainably cannot continue supporting users or communities. Holistic ROI therefore does not replace conventional ROI. It establishes what conventional ROI measures well and then asks what it leaves outside its boundary.

---

## 2. User Return

**User Return** is the value captured or created by people and organizations using the infrastructure. For broadband, that can include:

- lower telecommunications costs;
- remote employment;
- educational access;
- telehealth;
- reduced travel;
- business productivity;
- cloud services;
- entrepreneurship;
- access to markets;
- institutional efficiency;
- and improved service delivery.

The economic sequence is not simply:

> Infrastructure → Connection

It is:

> **Availability → Affordability → Adoption → Skills → Productive Use → User Return**

A connected address is therefore an infrastructure output. What the household, organization, or business can accomplish because it is connected begins to reveal the return.

---

## 3. Community Return

**Community Return** is value that accrues beyond an individual owner-user transaction to the surrounding community or regional economy. That can include:

- jobs;
- wages;
- locally retained income;
- business formation;
- private investment;
- local procurement;
- workforce capability;
- shared services;
- institutional capacity;
- greater resilience;
- reduced duplication;
- improved coordination;
- and stronger relationships among regional institutions.

Community Return is particularly important in rural economic development because infrastructure expenditures do not automatically remain local. If engineering, installation, construction, cybersecurity, maintenance, operations, and technical expertise must continually be purchased from outside the region, a substantial portion of the economic activity associated with infrastructure leaves the community. Investing in local capability can change that. The economic sequence then becomes:

> **Infrastructure Investment → Local Training → Local Capability → Employment and Firms → Locally Retained Income → Greater Regional Capacity**

Community Return therefore includes both economic activity and the capacity to retain more of that activity locally.

---

## 4. Opportunity Value

The fourth category is **Opportunity Value**.

> **Opportunity Value is the value of new choices, capabilities, investments, relationships, and productive activities made possible by an investment that would otherwise have been unavailable, less feasible, more expensive, or slower to develop.**

This includes conventional option value, but is intentionally broader. Opportunity Value can include:

- follow-on private investment;
- future public investment;
- new businesses;
- new services;
- new applications;
- workforce capabilities;
- institutional relationships;
- reusable data;
- reusable knowledge;
- shared infrastructure;
- reduced risk for subsequent investment;
- reduced cost of subsequent investment;
- and technologies or uses that were not foreseeable when the original investment was made.

Shapiro's treatment of telecommunications as "spillover-rich" infrastructure helps explain an economic mechanism underlying Opportunity Value. Infrastructure can support productive activity that the owner cannot completely predict, quantify, or internally monetize when making the original investment decision. ([Shapiro 2016a](https://quello.msu.edu/the-internet-as-spillover-rich-infrastructure/); [Shapiro 2016d](https://quello.msu.edu/btop-middle-mile-fiber-the-potential-of-generative-special-access/)) Opportunity Value extends this insight by asking:

> **How has the investment changed the set of feasible future actions available to people, institutions, businesses, investors, and communities?**

That question is particularly important when evaluating long-lived infrastructure. Part of the value of infrastructure may be the value of **not having to know the future in advance**.

# Applying Holistic ROI to EUPConnect

The EUPConnect Collaborative provides a useful environment in which to apply the framework because its work already crosses the boundaries between infrastructure, institutions, users, economic development, workforce development, and community capacity. The current draft traces a development sequence that includes broadband measurement, citizen science, approximately 70 miles of high-capacity middle-mile infrastructure through LIFT-UP, subsequent last-mile investment, digital-opportunity initiatives, workforce development, and a high-capacity regional education network. The regional development sequence can be represented as:

> **Measure → Organize → Invest → Connect → Build Capacity**

Holistic ROI asks what returns appear at each stage.

---

## Measuring Investment Leverage

EUPConnect's financial contribution is not equivalent to the total capital deployed around collaborative initiatives. That distinction creates an important measurement problem. A collaborative may contribute research, planning, convening, local match, technical assistance, advocacy, or institutional relationships that help make a larger investment feasible. The resulting external investment should not automatically be credited to the collaborative. But neither should the collaborative's role disappear from the analysis. A useful measure is:

> **Regional Leverage Ratio = Verified External Resources Mobilized ÷ EUPConnect Resources Committed**

Every investment should additionally record the nature of EUPConnect's contribution:

- directly secured;
- co-developed;
- matched or pooled;
- facilitated;
- supported;
- or regionally associated.

This preserves attribution discipline while allowing regional leverage to become measurable.

## Infrastructure as a Platform Rather Than the Return Itself

The physical infrastructure story in the Eastern Upper Peninsula is important because the region did not move from poor connectivity to a single completed broadband project. What has emerged instead is a succession of investments in planning, middle-mile capacity, last-mile networks, institutional connectivity, and resilience. Each layer has altered the economics of the layer that followed. That progression makes the EUP particularly useful for understanding Holistic ROI: the return from one investment often appears partly in the feasibility, cost, reach, or usefulness of the next investment.

The groundwork predates the formal creation of the EUPConnect Collaborative. Regional broadband planning supported by an Economic Development Administration Broadband Infrastructure Planning Grant was already underway by 2019, when partners were mapping infrastructure, examining routes and costs, and moving from localized connectivity problems toward a coordinated regional framework. An EUP broadband GIS workshop that year trained community partners to collect and analyze infrastructure and availability data using ArcGIS. The importance of that work was not the map alone. It was the development of shared evidence that schools, local governments, planners, providers, and other institutions could use to make infrastructure decisions together.

That evidence-building continued through the Michigan Moonshot partnership with Merit Network and the MSU Quello Center. Citizen-science research, broadband measurement, community surveys, and engagement helped the region identify gaps often obscured by broad federal availability classifications. The resulting picture mattered because the region's problem was not simply whether some form of Internet service existed. Analysis continued to show a geographic divide in which infrastructure outside more populated areas was more likely to deliver lower-quality service than infrastructure serving areas with greater housing and business density. Across Chippewa, Luce, and Mackinac counties, understanding that variation was essential to deciding where shared infrastructure could most significantly change deployment economics.

One of the most consequential physical investments to emerge from this regional trajectory was LIFT-UP. Merit Network received a $2.99 million Economic Development Administration award supporting construction of approximately 70 miles of high-capacity middle-mile fiber in the Upper Peninsula. EUPConnect materials describe that investment as a shared backbone rather than an endpoint: strategic middle mile created infrastructure upon which last-mile providers and later public and private investments could build. In practical terms, middle-mile fiber can shorten the distance a provider must finance independently to reach a rural community, create alternative routes and interconnection possibilities, and make locations that were previously marginal more feasible to serve.

### A Portfolio of Overlapping Infrastructure Investment

The last-mile story is broader than a single sequence of LIFT-UP, Highline, and E-Rate. It is better understood as a **portfolio of overlapping infrastructure investments**, each addressing different gaps in geography, ownership, network architecture, or eligible service territory. That portfolio includes Highline's RDOF- and ROBIN-supported expansion, Spectrum's ROBIN-supported construction, the Sault Ste. Marie Tribe of Chippewa Indians' ReConnect 3 and ReConnect 4 investments, Enhanced A-CAM-supported work associated regionally with Michigan Broadband Services/Upper Peninsula Telephone Company and Hiawatha/Jamadots, BEAD, private provider capital, and the emerging regional E-Rate education network. The significance of these programs is not simply their individual scale. Their combined effect is changing the underlying network geography of the EUP.

Highline's expansion illustrates how one funding mechanism can fill gaps around infrastructure financed through another. Highline has extended fiber through communities including Pickford, Cedarville, and toward DeTour, using support that spans RDOF, ROBIN, and planned BEAD work in different areas. Michigan's [ROBIN award table](https://www.michigan.gov/leo/bureaus-agencies/mihi/funding-opportunities/robin-grant) lists Highline/Aspire's Upper Peninsula project across eight counties, including Chippewa, Luce, and Mackinac, with 1,243 funded locations and an $11.2 million award. Reaching DeTour required more than connecting an isolated endpoint; the route created alternative infrastructure through communities historically served by few options. That is Opportunity Value in physical form: a route financed for one set of premises changes the feasibility of serving other households, institutions, and future applications along the way.

Spectrum's ROBIN-supported build is a second material layer and should be treated separately from Highline's work. The same state award table lists Spectrum's Eastern Chippewa County project at 1,979 funded locations and approximately $15.87 million, covering work associated regionally with Sugar Island, Bruce, Pickford, Soo Township, and Neebish Island. EUPConnect records show construction beginning in Bruce Township in 2026, and [November 2026 reporting](https://upnorthlive.com/news/local/spectrum-project-to-bring-high-speed-internet-to-two-up-islands-by-end-of-year-chippewa-county-sugar-island-neebish-island) described directional boring beneath the St. Marys River as part of the effort to connect Sugar and Neebish islands. The technical output is fiber reaching difficult geography; the broader return appears through telehealth, distance learning, business activity, educational access, public services, and the improved feasibility of future island and mainland connections.

The Sault Ste. Marie Tribe of Chippewa Indians adds a major Tribal infrastructure layer. USDA's [fiscal year 2022 ReConnect award record](https://www.usda.gov/sustainability/infrastructure/broadband/reconnect-loan-and-grant-program/reconnect-announcements/reconnect-program-fy-2022-funding-opportunity-announcement-awardees) identifies a $24,944,355 ReConnect 3 investment to deploy fiber to the premises for 403 households, 1,217 people, and 26 businesses in Chippewa and Mackinac counties. Regional implementation records describe the corridor from St. Ignace to Sault Ste. Marie and identify sites intended to be open for transport by other Internet service providers, with meet points envisioned along the middle-mile route. That design matters to Holistic ROI because Tribal fiber serving its original premises can also create transport and interconnection options that improve the economics of networks owned by others.

ReConnect 4 should be represented as a second Sault Tribe investment rather than collapsing the Tribal build into one award. USDA's [fiscal year 2023 ReConnect award materials](https://www.usda.gov/sustainability/infrastructure/broadband/reconnect-loan-and-grant-program/reconnect-announcements/reconnect-program-fy-2023-funding-opportunity-announcement-awardees) list the Sault Sainte Marie Tribe of Chippewa Indians among Michigan recipients. Keeping ReConnect 3 and ReConnect 4 distinct protects the article from assigning one round's route, award amount, premises count, or construction status to the other. Together, however, the two awards demonstrate how Tribal investment can create both direct User Return for connected households and businesses and wider Opportunity Value through transport, interconnection, and the possibility of future Tribal or wholesale network services.

Enhanced A-CAM introduces a different economic mechanism into the same regional portfolio. Established by the FCC in 2023, [Enhanced A-CAM](https://www.usac.org/high-cost/funds/enhanced-acam/) provides roughly $18.28 billion over 15 years to participating rural rate-of-return carriers, with obligations to deploy at least 100/20 Mbps service to required locations and maintain or improve qualifying service at other locations by the end of 2028. Unlike ROBIN or ReConnect, Enhanced A-CAM is not a one-time competitive capital grant. It is long-term universal-service support tied to incumbent-carrier study areas and deployment obligations. That distinction strengthens the Holistic ROI case because regional infrastructure is being assembled through different financial structures, not merely through different grant names.

Michigan Broadband Services belongs in that story through its relationship with Upper Peninsula Telephone Company and the incumbent network it operates in the region. EUPConnect records describe fiber-to-the-premises progress on Drummond Island and identify Michigan Broadband Services/UPTel as owners of existing fiber and copper assets that shape last-mile and middle-mile options. A 2026 [FCC order](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-26-181A1.txt) identifies Upper Peninsula Telephone Company as a Michigan rate-of-return carrier that elected fixed support under Enhanced A-CAM. The relevant return is therefore not limited to the carrier's supported deployment obligation. Existing routes, interconnection, and upgraded access infrastructure can also lower the cost of later extensions, support institutional demand, and give other regional projects a stronger platform from which to proceed.

Hiawatha's Enhanced A-CAM layer should be represented on the same terms. Regional records identify Hiawatha/Jamadots Enhanced A-CAM activity in western Chippewa County and northern Luce County and separately document fiber-to-the-home construction along M-28 and in Dafter and Superior townships. Hiawatha also sits within a broader transport context that includes affiliated telecommunications companies and relationships with Peninsula Fiber Network. Treating this work only as generic incumbent construction would miss the financing structure and network relationships behind it. In Holistic ROI terms, the supported access build can produce Owner Return for the carrier while generating User Return, Community Return, and Opportunity Value through improved reach, redundancy, and the ability of institutions and later projects to use the resulting network.

The next major layer is institutional connectivity. The EUP E-Rate Consortium has been developing a regional education network that would substantially increase capacity available to participating schools. The selected Merit proposal provides 10 Gbps connections to sites and 100 Gbps at hub locations, creating a 100 Gbps regional ring. The project combines E-Rate support, state and federal matching opportunities, MiSEN participation, EUPConnect resources, and Merit infrastructure. EUPConnect is the applicant for the E-Rate discounts and committed $200,000 in direct project funding, while EUPISD serves as fiscal agent and MiSEN has also contributed financial and application support. The infrastructure is expected to serve roughly 6,000 students across the Eastern Upper Peninsula.

This education network illustrates why infrastructure output and infrastructure return should not be confused. A 100 Gbps ring is an impressive technical output, but bandwidth is not the ultimate return. The return begins to appear when schools can share services, students gain access to opportunities that geography previously constrained, districts adopt applications that would have been impractical on lower-capacity connections, and the same architecture creates options for future educational, workforce, research, community, or emergency-service applications. Capacity installed ahead of immediate demand also carries Opportunity Value because it reduces the likelihood that the region must rebuild foundational transport every time a new application emerges.

The emerging infrastructure system must also be understood in the context of the EUP's unusual geography. The region includes an international border, island communities, long rural routes, extensive forested areas, and constrained middle-mile and backhaul paths. EUPConnect's 2026 NTIA comments identify cross-border radio-frequency conditions, limited terrestrial redundancy, island connectivity, and constrained routes as complications for 911 and emergency communications as well as ordinary broadband deployment. The same comments identify nearly 300 critical facilities and argue that stronger interconnection among schools, Tribal facilities, healthcare institutions, public-safety organizations, higher education, and energy facilities would improve emergency communications while creating anchor demand for broader network investment.

Taken together, these investments change the story of broadband development in the Eastern Upper Peninsula. The region is not being connected through one public network or one private provider. It is being reshaped through a **capital stack distributed across many infrastructure owners and public programs**: EDA-supported middle mile, RDOF, ROBIN, USDA ReConnect 3 and ReConnect 4, Enhanced A-CAM, BEAD, private provider capital, Tribal investment, and E-Rate-supported institutional transport. Each mechanism solves a somewhat different economic problem, and each creates infrastructure that can alter the feasibility or value of investments made by someone else.

The infrastructure should consequently be understood as an emerging regional system rather than a list of grants. Earlier investments change the economics and possibilities of later ones: middle mile can improve the economics of last-mile construction; last-mile construction increases the number of households and institutions able to use regional backbones; institutional demand improves utilization; and new routes introduce alternatives and resilience in places that previously had few options. The resulting chain is better understood as:

> **Evidence → Middle Mile → Last Mile → Institutional Networks → Productive Use → Regional Capacity → New Investment**

At each stage, the location of the return changes. Spectrum may calculate Owner Return on its ROBIN-supported network differently from the Sault Tribe's calculation for ReConnect infrastructure, Michigan Broadband Services' or Hiawatha's economics under Enhanced A-CAM, Highline's economics across RDOF and ROBIN territories, or Merit's economics around middle-mile and institutional transport. Yet households, schools, healthcare providers, businesses, Tribal institutions, emergency services, and communities use and benefit from the resulting infrastructure regardless of which program financed a particular segment. **Holistic ROI provides a way to see those separate capital projects as components of one regional opportunity system.**

That is precisely where Owner Return, User Return, Community Return, and Opportunity Value diverge. The physical infrastructure is the common platform, but the value it creates is distributed across owners, users, communities, and future investments.

# Fund Stacking as ROI Engineering

This distributed-benefit problem has a corresponding investment implication.

> **When returns are distributed, investment can be distributed too.**

This turns fund stacking from an opportunistic grant strategy into a more coherent form of **ROI engineering**. When multiple organizations capture different parts of an infrastructure investment's value, it can be economically rational for multiple organizations or funding programs to participate in financing it. The goal is to align the:

> **capital stack with the benefit stack.**

EUPConnect's regional education-network planning already illustrates this logic through combinations of local contribution, E-Rate assistance, state and federal support, and partner resources. A later project announcement provides a particularly relevant financing precedent. In 2021, the Michigan State University Center for Community and Economic Development announced a Shapiro-led project to develop a grant proposal for an in-depth study of Automated Open Access fiber networks. The announcement described networks that could support competing Internet providers over shared infrastructure and linked the model to telehealth, remote learning, smart-grid services, smart-city services, security, privacy, and innovation. It also identified the possibility of financing deployment in ways that reflect value distributed across those uses and public services. This source is a project announcement—not a completed feasibility study—so the article uses it for the financing rationale it states, not as evidence that the model produced those outcomes. ([MSU CCED 2021](https://reicenter.org/upload/ceri/Shapiro%20Press%20Release.pdf)) That logic is closely aligned with Holistic ROI.

If infrastructure produces Owner Return, User Return, Community Return, and Opportunity Value across different organizations, expecting a single beneficiary to finance the full system may systematically understate the investment's economic viability. Fund stacking becomes a mechanism for allowing financing to better reflect the actual distribution of benefit.

# Coordination as Community Return

One of EUPConnect's largest potential returns may not be physical infrastructure. Every rural organization faces transaction costs. Without shared regional capacity, school districts, municipalities, Tribal governments, healthcare organizations, nonprofits, economic-development organizations, and other institutions may separately need to:

- interpret infrastructure data;
- understand funding programs;
- develop relationships with providers;
- locate technical expertise;
- conduct planning;
- prepare grant applications;
- establish partnerships;
- communicate with state and federal agencies;
- and continually reconstruct knowledge already developed elsewhere in the region.

Shared capacity can reduce those costs. This is a form of **Community Return**. Possible measures include:

| Indicator | Measurement Method |
|---|---|
| Avoided duplication | Staff or consultant time avoided through shared work |
| Shared research | Organizations reusing regional data, plans, studies, or analysis |
| Procurement leverage | Collaborative cost compared with stand-alone procurement |
| Project acceleration | Time from identified need to funding, award, or construction |
| Grant efficiency | Shared effort, applications, and resulting awards |
| Knowledge reuse | Subsequent projects drawing upon earlier work |
| Partner introductions | Relationships resulting in projects, investment, or resources |
| Relationship persistence | Partnerships continuing beyond the initiating project |

Making these effects visible makes regional coordination **economically legible**.

# Workforce and the Regional Capture of Return

Infrastructure spending does not necessarily translate into equivalent regional economic development. The important question is how much value the region retains. If engineering, construction, installation, cybersecurity, controls, network operations, and maintenance must continually be imported, much of the expenditure immediately leaves the region. That creates a useful metric for Community Return:

> **Regional Capture Rate: the proportion of infrastructure-related spending, wages, contracts, expertise, and subsequent economic activity retained within the region.**

Workforce development can increase this capture rate. The chain becomes:

> **Infrastructure Investment → Local Training → Local Capability → Employment and Firms → Locally Retained Income → Greater Regional Capacity**

This reframes workforce development. It is not simply a social benefit attached to infrastructure investment. It is an economic mechanism for retaining a greater portion of the return produced by investment.

---

# Measuring Opportunity Value

Opportunity Value requires a different measurement strategy because it unfolds over time. Projects should therefore be revisited longitudinally at intervals such as:

- project completion;
- 12 months;
- 36 months;
- and 60 months.

At each interval, ask:

> **What exists now that would probably not exist, or would have been materially more difficult to create, without the earlier investment?**

Evidence might include:

- new private investment;
- a new business;
- a workforce program;
- an infrastructure extension;
- a new partnership;
- a subsequent grant;
- a shared service;
- a curriculum;
- a new technology deployment;
- research enabled by earlier data;
- or a new institution.

This creates an **investment genealogy**. Instead of treating each grant, infrastructure project, and program as an isolated event, the genealogy records which subsequent investments and capabilities depend upon earlier ones. That may ultimately become one of the most important measures in Holistic ROI.

---

# Measuring Holistic ROI

A credible Holistic ROI methodology should combine multiple types of evidence rather than forcing every effect into a dollar value.

## Establish Baselines

Before major investments, document relevant conditions such as:

- infrastructure availability;
- adoption;
- capacity;
- recurring costs;
- outages;
- workforce vacancies;
- local skills;
- external consultant spending;
- institutional relationships;
- economic participation;
- and existing public or private investment.

## Maintain an Investment Ledger

For each major initiative, record:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Project | Defines the intervention |
| Owner investment | Identifies asset-owner capital |
| Local investment | Tracks regional contribution |
| State/federal investment | Tracks public leverage |
| Partner investment | Tracks institutional participation |
| Private investment | Tracks market response |
| In-kind resources | Captures noncash contribution |
| Infrastructure output | Records what was built |
| Beneficiaries | Identifies who receives value |
| EUPConnect role | Supports appropriate attribution |
| Subsequent investment | Begins measuring Opportunity Value |

## Measure Owner Return

Depending on the project:

- revenue;
- operating savings;
- utilization;
- lifecycle cost;
- reliability;
- avoided maintenance;
- and financial sustainability.

## Measure User Return

Potential indicators include:

- affordability;
- adoption;
- productivity;
- remote employment;
- digital-skills use;
- educational use;
- telehealth;
- business activity;
- avoided travel;
- and institutional efficiency.

## Measure Community Return

Potential indicators include:

- jobs;
- wages;
- locally retained spending;
- credentials;
- businesses created or expanded;
- private investment;
- local procurement;
- shared services;
- coordination efficiency;
- institutional capacity;
- resilience;
- and persistent regional partnerships.

## Measure Opportunity Value

Track:

- follow-on investment;
- projects that reuse previous infrastructure;
- programs made feasible by previous investments;
- relationships created through earlier projects;
- new applications;
- new businesses;
- subsequent grants;
- knowledge reuse;
- and capabilities reducing the cost or risk of future action.

---

# Measurement Without Overclaiming

Holistic ROI requires disciplined attribution. Regional development is collaborative. The presence of EUPConnect in a project does not prove that every resulting investment or outcome was caused by EUPConnect. Evidence should therefore distinguish among:

> **EUPConnect caused → EUPConnect contributed → EUPConnect facilitated → EUPConnect supported → EUPConnect participated → regionally associated**

Where appropriate, stronger evaluation methods can include:

- before-and-after comparisons;
- matched-region or matched-community analysis;
- interrupted time series;
- difference-in-differences;
- contribution analysis;
- process tracing;
- and stakeholder counterfactual interviews.

The defining counterfactual remains:

> **If EUPConnect had not existed, what would have happened differently?**

That is more demanding than counting projects with which the collaborative was associated. It asks what distinctive regional capacity the collaborative actually created.

---

# The Holistic ROI Scorecard

The resulting framework can be summarized in four return accounts.

| Return | Core Question | Representative Measures |
|---|---|---|
| **Owner Return** | What does the owner or operator gain? | Revenue, savings, utilization, reliability, avoided costs, lifecycle value |
| **User Return** | What do users gain or create? | Productivity, affordability, access, education, telehealth, employment, business use |
| **Community Return** | What does the wider community gain? | Jobs, wages, local procurement, workforce capability, resilience, coordination, institutional capacity |
| **Opportunity Value** | What becomes possible next? | Follow-on investment, new businesses, services, partnerships, capabilities, applications, infrastructure and knowledge reuse |

This scorecard intentionally avoids reducing all outcomes to a single economic multiplier. Different forms of value require different evidence. What matters is that they appear within the same investment analysis.

---

# EUPConnect as a Regional Return Catalyst

This framework suggests a clearer way of describing EUPConnect's economic function. EUPConnect does not need to own every asset through which regional value is produced. Its role does not necessarily need to be **capturing the return**. Its more consequential role may be helping the regional system **produce more return from the resources already present or being invested**. The sequence becomes:

> **Measure → Organize → Pool → Align → De-risk → Invest → Connect → Develop → Retain → Create Opportunity**

EUPConnect helps make needs visible. It organizes institutions possessing different resources. It creates opportunities to pool resources where interests overlap. It helps align otherwise disconnected investments. It can reduce information and coordination barriers. Partners then invest. Infrastructure connects people and institutions. Users create value through that infrastructure. Workforce and institutional development help retain more of the resulting value locally. And previous investments create opportunities for subsequent ones. The cycle begins again at a higher level of regional capability.

---

# From Financial ROI to Holistic ROI

Traditional infrastructure ROI can be represented as:

> **Investment → Asset → Revenue → Return**

Holistic ROI broadens the system:

> **Investment → Infrastructure → Owner Return + User Return + Community Return → Opportunity Value → Follow-on Investment → New Capability**

The change is not an argument against financial discipline. The owner still requires a sustainable return. Rather, Holistic ROI recognizes that the owner is only one location in which infrastructure value can appear. That distinction helps explain why economically important infrastructure can remain underprovided in rural communities. If the party expected to finance the investment can capture only a fraction of the total value produced, conventional project-level ROI can systematically underestimate the economic case for investment. Holistic ROI expands the measurement boundary. Fund stacking and resource pooling expand the investment boundary accordingly.

---

# Conclusion

The central problem with conventional infrastructure ROI may not be that it is wrong. It may simply draw the economic boundary too narrowly. Mitchell Shapiro's work provides an important foundation for understanding why. It moves from spillover-rich infrastructure and ownership design to public fiber, rural electric cooperative broadband, regional broadband ecosystems, and a proposal to study Automated Open Access networks. Across that progression is a consistent concern with value that extends beyond the revenue directly captured by the network owner. ([Shapiro 2016a](https://quello.msu.edu/the-internet-as-spillover-rich-infrastructure/); [Shapiro 2016c](https://quello.msu.edu/community-broadband-as-generative-infrastructure/); [Shapiro 2016d](https://quello.msu.edu/btop-middle-mile-fiber-the-potential-of-generative-special-access/); [Shapiro 2019](https://reicenter.org/upload/documents/colearning/Electric%20Cooperatives%20and%20the%20Digital%20Divide.pdf); [Shapiro 2020](https://reicenter.org/upload/documents/MShapiro_2020Fellowship_FinalReport.pdf); [MSU CCED 2021](https://reicenter.org/upload/ceri/Shapiro%20Press%20Release.pdf)) Holistic ROI carries that reasoning into a four-category framework. **Owner Return** recognizes that infrastructure must produce sustainable value for whoever owns and operates it.

**User Return** recognizes that households, businesses, and institutions create and capture additional value through its use. **Community Return** recognizes that some benefits emerge at the regional level through employment, workforce capability, institutional capacity, resilience, coordination, and locally retained economic activity. **Opportunity Value** recognizes that perhaps the most consequential effect of an investment is that it changes what becomes possible next. This distinction has practical consequences for economic development. If infrastructure produces value across multiple beneficiaries, expecting a single beneficiary to finance the entire investment can cause viable investments to appear uneconomic. When benefits are distributed, investment can be distributed too.

Fund stacking is therefore more than a mechanism for closing a project budget. Resource pooling is more than a response to scarcity. Collaboration is more than an administrative arrangement. Together they can form an investment architecture that more closely reflects the actual distribution of value created by enabling infrastructure. For the Eastern Upper Peninsula, this changes the definition of success. Success is not simply miles of fiber, dollars awarded, addresses reached, meetings held, or programs completed. Those are important outputs, but they are not the final return. The larger question is whether those investments leave the region **more capable than it was before**: more connected, more skilled, better coordinated, more resilient, more capable of retaining economic value locally, and better positioned to recognize and act upon the next opportunity. That leads to the central proposition of Holistic ROI:

> **Traditional ROI measures what an investment returns. Holistic ROI also measures what the investment makes possible.**

And for EUPConnect, the ultimate question becomes:

> **Did today's investment solve today's problem, or did it also expand what the Eastern Upper Peninsula can do tomorrow?**

If that second effect can be measured with the same discipline traditionally applied to financial return, EUPConnect can move beyond telling a compelling story of regional collaboration. It can begin demonstrating the **Holistic Return on Investment of regional capacity itself**.
