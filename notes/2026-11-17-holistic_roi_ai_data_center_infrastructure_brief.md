# Holistic ROI for AI and Data-Center Infrastructure

## From “Who Pays?” to “Who Benefits?”

## Executive Summary

Artificial intelligence and data-center expansion are creating a new infrastructure bargain. Private facilities need enormous quantities of electricity, transmission capacity, land, communications infrastructure, and public permitting capacity. Conventional return-on-investment analysis can show whether a project works for its owner, but it does not adequately reveal when enabling costs are transferred to utility customers or when public infrastructure creates benefits that remain concentrated within a private facility.

The **Holistic ROI framework** addresses this limitation by evaluating four complementary forms of return: **Owner Return, User Return, Community Return, and Opportunity Value**. Applied to AI and data-center infrastructure, the framework shifts the central question from how much investment a project attracts to **who bears its costs, who captures its benefits, and what durable capabilities remain after it is built**.

### Key findings

1. **An attractive Owner Return can conceal public cost shifting.** Proposed federal legislation described by Brad Plumer would accelerate infrastructure permitting while directing the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission to develop rules requiring data centers to pay for grid upgrades needed to serve them and for their use of existing transmission infrastructure.[^1] Grid Strategies estimated that these federal provisions could shift approximately **$35 billion in costs to data centers over ten years**.[^1]

2. **Ratepayer protection is a form of User Return.** Electricity customers are infrastructure users. If the incremental costs created by exceptionally large new loads are spread across households and existing businesses, a project may generate strong Owner Return while producing weak or negative User Return. Holistic ROI therefore treats affordability, reliability, and protection from inappropriate cost allocation as measurable user outcomes.

3. **Community Return should be measured as durable local capacity, not announced investment.** Grid upgrades, regional fiber, workforce credentials, community-college partnerships, resilience assets, tax capacity, and household bill protections are more meaningful measures than capital expenditure alone. State-level mechanisms examined by Grid Strategies could potentially generate an additional **$136 billion**, including revenue that regulators could use to offset household electricity costs.[^1]

4. **Opportunity Value distinguishes dedicated infrastructure from generative infrastructure.** A transmission line, fiber route, or energy system serving only one facility creates comparatively narrow future value. Infrastructure designed with interoperable capacity for additional businesses, institutions, housing, distributed energy, education, or community resilience expands the region’s future choices.

5. **Cost-allocation policy can produce unintended environmental trade-offs.** Higher grid charges or long interconnection delays may encourage behind-the-meter fossil generation. Rhodium Group estimated that serving future data-center growth with off-grid gas could produce **31 to 90 percent more greenhouse-gas emissions** than serving the same demand through the interconnected grid.[^1] Holistic ROI makes this trade-off visible by evaluating immediate ratepayer protection alongside environmental performance and long-term Opportunity Value.

6. **The framework should guide project design and negotiation, not merely score completed proposals.** Communities, utilities, and developers can use the four returns to structure cost-allocation agreements, infrastructure-sharing provisions, workforce commitments, energy standards, and community-benefit mechanisms before scarce public and grid capacity is committed.

The resulting policy principle is straightforward:

> **Large infrastructure investments should internalize the costs they create, distribute benefits deliberately, and leave behind capabilities that expand the community’s future choices.**

## The Holistic ROI Framework

The rapid growth of artificial intelligence is forcing communities, utilities, regulators, and technology companies to confront a question that conventional return-on-investment analysis handles poorly: **When a private infrastructure investment creates substantial public costs and benefits, what constitutes a good return?**

The emerging debate over data-center electricity provides a useful test of the **Holistic ROI framework**, which evaluates investment through four complementary forms of return: **Owner Return, User Return, Community Return, and Opportunity Value**. Rather than treating externalities as secondary considerations, Holistic ROI asks whether the economic architecture surrounding an investment distributes its costs, benefits, capabilities, and future options in ways that create durable value.

A November 2026 report by Brad Plumer in *The New York Times* describes proposed federal permitting legislation that illustrates why this broader accounting matters. The legislation would accelerate permitting for infrastructure including transmission and energy projects, potentially making it easier to supply rapidly growing AI data centers. At the same time, it would require large data centers to shoulder substantially more of the infrastructure costs their electricity demand creates.[^1]

That combination suggests an important principle for Holistic ROI:

> **Infrastructure investment should be evaluated not simply by how much value it creates, but by who captures that value, who bears the enabling costs, and what new capabilities the investment leaves behind.**

## The Four Returns

### 1. Owner Return: Does the investment work for the investor?

Traditional ROI remains essential. A data center must produce sufficient value to justify capital expenditures, electricity, cooling, connectivity, land, labor, financing, and operating risk.

AI infrastructure complicates that calculation because electricity increasingly becomes both a cost and a constraint. The proposed federal legislation would direct the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission to establish rules requiring data centers to pay not only for grid upgrades necessary to serve them but also additional costs associated with their use of existing transmission infrastructure.[^1]

An analysis by Grid Strategies, cited by *The New York Times*, estimates that the federal provisions alone could shift roughly **$35 billion in grid costs to data centers over ten years**.[^1]

Those costs change Owner Return, but they also expose something conventional ROI tends to hide: some apparently attractive private returns can depend upon infrastructure costs being absorbed elsewhere.

Holistic ROI therefore does not replace Owner Return. It **makes its boundaries visible**.

### 2. User Return: Does the infrastructure improve experience, capability, or affordability?

The second return belongs to people and organizations actually using the resulting infrastructure and services.

For AI infrastructure, User Return can include better computing availability, faster digital services, greater reliability, lower latency, new educational and research capabilities, and improved access to computational resources.

Electricity customers are users of infrastructure too.

Historically, utilities have often spread transmission and generation investments across broad classes of customers. The proposed legislation represents a different approach for exceptionally large loads: incremental infrastructure costs associated with data centers could increasingly be assigned to those facilities rather than passed through to households and other customers.[^1]

That turns **ratepayer protection into a measurable component of User Return**.

A project that generates an excellent financial return for its owner while materially raising electricity costs for existing residents may have strong Owner Return but poor User Return.

Holistic ROI makes that imbalance visible rather than averaging it away.

### 3. Community Return: What does the place receive in exchange?

This is where the data-center debate becomes particularly relevant to rural communities.

Communities contribute more than a development site. They provide, directly or indirectly, access to electricity systems, roads, water, telecommunications, permitting capacity, public institutions, natural resources, and sometimes tax incentives.

The appropriate question is therefore not merely:

**How much investment is coming into the community?**

It is:

**What durable community assets result from that investment?**

Community Return might include:

- transmission or distribution improvements benefiting other customers;
- broadband and fiber infrastructure with usable excess capacity;
- workforce training and recognized credentials;
- apprenticeships and local employment pathways;
- partnerships with community colleges and K–12 systems;
- distributed-energy and resilience investments;
- increased local tax capacity;
- infrastructure that supports future businesses rather than serving one facility exclusively; and
- mechanisms protecting existing customers from infrastructure costs generated primarily by a new large load.

The proposed federal legislation provides an unusually concrete example. Besides the estimated $35 billion associated with federal grid provisions, Grid Strategies estimated that state-level provisions could potentially generate another **$136 billion** if fully utilized, including mechanisms through which regulators could offset household electricity costs.[^1]

This shifts the conversation from vague notions of corporate community benefit toward something measurable:

> **Community Return is the portion of an infrastructure investment’s value that becomes durable community capacity.**

### 4. Opportunity Value: What becomes possible afterward?

The fourth category may be the most important for evaluating infrastructure.

**Opportunity Value measures the additional choices and capabilities created by an investment, including benefits that cannot yet be assigned to a specific future project.**

A transmission improvement that only powers one data center has comparatively narrow Opportunity Value. A grid-modernization project that also enables new industrial loads, distributed generation, storage, electric transportation, housing development, or community microgrids creates options extending beyond the original investment.

The same reasoning applies to communications infrastructure. Fiber serving an AI facility becomes more valuable from a Holistic ROI perspective when its architecture can also strengthen regional middle-mile connectivity, institutional networks, education, healthcare, businesses, or future community infrastructure.

This is the difference between **infrastructure as an input** and **infrastructure as a platform**.

The distinction becomes especially important because raising grid charges could produce unintended behavior. *The New York Times* reports that some data-center developers are already considering off-grid generation using gas turbines and repurposed jet engines because connecting to the electrical grid can take years.[^1]

An analysis from Rhodium Group cited in the article estimated that satisfying future data-center growth using off-grid gas could produce **31 to 90 percent more greenhouse-gas emissions** than serving the same loads from the interconnected grid.[^1]

A policy that improves Community Return through ratepayer protection could therefore unintentionally reduce environmental or long-term Opportunity Value if it pushes development toward isolated fossil generation.

That is precisely the kind of trade-off Holistic ROI is intended to expose.

## A Practical Holistic ROI Test

For a major AI, data-center, energy, broadband, or industrial infrastructure proposal, decision makers can ask four questions:

| Dimension | Core question | Example measures |
|---|---|---|
| **Owner Return** | Does the investment create sustainable value for its owner? | Net present value, internal rate of return, operating costs, energy costs, utilization, time to energization |
| **User Return** | Do users receive better service, capability, reliability, or affordability? | Electricity rates, reliability, latency, service availability, access to computing resources |
| **Community Return** | What durable value remains in the community? | Local jobs, tax base, shared infrastructure, credentials, apprenticeships, ratepayer protection, resilience assets |
| **Opportunity Value** | What additional things become possible because this was built? | Spare capacity, interoperability, resilience, future projects, institutional capability, new development options |

The goal is **not to maximize every category independently**. There will inevitably be trade-offs.

The better objective is to determine whether an investment creates an acceptable return **across the whole system** and whether one participant’s return depends on shifting disproportionate costs to another.

## From Impact Assessment to Infrastructure Design

This distinction matters because Holistic ROI should not merely become another scorecard applied after plans are complete.

It should influence **how infrastructure is designed in the first place**.

Consider two hypothetical $1 billion data-center projects. Both might generate identical financial returns and computing capacity. But suppose one requires customers to subsidize utility upgrades, creates dedicated infrastructure that cannot support additional development, imports most specialized labor, and relies on isolated fossil generation.

The second pays its incremental grid costs, finances transmission with capacity available for subsequent development, partners with local educational institutions to build technical capability, expands regional fiber, participates in grid demand-response programs, and leaves infrastructure capable of supporting future industries.

Traditional project ROI might regard them as equivalent investments.

**Holistic ROI would not.**

The second project has transformed a private capital investment into **generative infrastructure**: infrastructure whose value extends beyond its original purpose and makes subsequent activity easier.

That provides a particularly useful principle for rural regions:

> **The best infrastructure investment does not merely solve today’s constraint. It expands tomorrow’s choices.**

The current data-center electricity debate therefore represents something larger than a dispute about utility rates. It is an early attempt to renegotiate the social and economic architecture surrounding AI infrastructure: who finances it, who benefits from it, what burdens are shared, and what capabilities remain afterward.

Holistic ROI supplies a framework for answering those questions before communities commit scarce capacity, rather than calculating the consequences afterward.
