# EUPConnect Policy Brief

## The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026: A Holistic ROI Perspective for the Eastern Upper Peninsula

**Draft | November 2026**

## Executive Summary

The **Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (BAAJA), S. 5653**, deserves close attention in Michigan's Eastern Upper Peninsula because it brings together several issues that are too often considered separately: infrastructure permitting, electric transmission, energy development, large computational loads such as data centers, and household affordability.

Introduced September 30, 2026, the bipartisan legislation proposes changes across seven titles addressing the National Environmental Policy Act, Clean Water Act, Endangered Species Act, project certainty, electric transmission, energy permitting, and historic preservation.[^1][^2]

For EUPConnect, the appropriate question is not simply whether these reforms will produce *more infrastructure*. The better question is whether the infrastructure produces durable value for the people and communities that host, use, finance, and depend upon it.

EUPConnect's **Holistic ROI framework** provides a useful way to answer that question. It evaluates infrastructure investment through four interconnected forms of return:

1. **Owner Return**: Does the investment work economically for the organization building or operating it?
2. **User Return**: Does it improve affordability, reliability, quality, or access for the people and organizations using the infrastructure?
3. **Community Return**: Does the investment create durable local economic and social value?
4. **Opportunity Value**: What new possibilities become viable because the infrastructure exists?

Applied to BAAJA, this framework points toward an important principle for future energy and computational infrastructure development in the Eastern Upper Peninsula:

> **Infrastructure should not merely be easier to build. It should create measurable value for the communities that enable it.**

## Why BAAJA Matters to the Eastern Upper Peninsula

BAAJA is fundamentally an infrastructure-enabling proposal. Its provisions are intended to shorten or clarify portions of federal environmental review and permitting while addressing transmission development and energy permitting. The Congressional Research Service identifies seven major legislative areas, including NEPA, Clean Water Act and Endangered Species Act provisions, project certainty, electric transmission, energy permitting, and historic preservation.[^1]

That combination matters in the EUP.

The region sits at the intersection of abundant natural resources, significant existing energy and communications infrastructure, substantial distances between communities, constrained transmission capacity, rural affordability challenges, tribal interests, and growing demand for digital infrastructure. Development of generation, transmission, broadband, advanced manufacturing, or computational infrastructure therefore cannot be evaluated solely according to construction cost or private investment.

The infrastructure changes the system around it.

That makes the question of **who receives the return** just as important as the question of whether a project can be permitted.

## Applying Holistic ROI

### 1. Owner Return

Infrastructure must first be economically sustainable for the organizations investing capital.

For an electric utility, transmission developer, generation owner, broadband provider, industrial customer, or data-center operator, that means examining capital expenditure, operating costs, financing, utilization, reliability, revenue, and long-term asset value.

Permitting reform could improve Owner Return by decreasing one of infrastructure development's largest uncertainties: **time**.

Long and unpredictable development processes increase financing costs, expose projects to changing market conditions, and make marginal projects more difficult to finance. Greater permitting certainty could therefore make investments in generation, transmission, and other infrastructure more viable.

But positive Owner Return alone is not sufficient justification for development.

A project capable of generating excellent returns for its owner while transferring infrastructure costs or risks to local ratepayers represents an incomplete return on investment from EUPConnect's perspective.

That distinction becomes especially important with large computational loads.

### 2. User Return

The second question is what infrastructure does for the people and organizations actually using the system.

For electricity customers, User Return can appear as:

- lower or more stable rates;
- improved reliability;
- additional generation capacity;
- reduced congestion;
- increased resilience;
- better service for businesses and institutions; and
- greater ability to electrify transportation, heating, or industrial processes.

BAAJA becomes particularly interesting here because the Administration's statement supporting S. 5653 explicitly connects the legislation with protecting ratepayers from the infrastructure costs associated with hyperscale computational loads. It states that data centers connecting to the grid at scale would bear their transmission costs rather than having those costs broadly socialized to households and small businesses.[^3]

That establishes an important policy principle:

> **A new load should not automatically become an old customer's bill.**

For rural regions, this principle matters enormously.

A hyperscale data center can consume electricity on the scale of major industrial development. Serving that demand may require new substations, transmission facilities, generation resources, interconnections, or system upgrades.

If those investments primarily exist to serve a particular large customer, cost allocation becomes part of the affordability conversation.

EUPConnect should therefore evaluate future large-load development according to both economic-development benefits **and its effect on existing electricity customers**.

### 3. Community Return

Infrastructure occupies a place.

Transmission corridors cross communities. Generation facilities use land and natural resources. Data centers connect to local electric, telecommunications, transportation, and sometimes water systems.

Communities therefore contribute something to infrastructure development even when they are not writing the construction check.

Holistic ROI asks what they receive in return.

Community Return could include:

- local construction and permanent employment;
- expansion of the local tax base;
- apprenticeship and workforce-training opportunities;
- investment in schools, colleges, libraries, and training centers;
- local procurement;
- telecommunications improvements;
- electric-system resilience;
- community infrastructure;
- brownfield redevelopment;
- support for housing and workforce development; and
- environmental restoration or stewardship.

This is where EUPConnect can introduce an important distinction between **economic activity and community capacity**.

A billion-dollar infrastructure project located in a community does not necessarily create a billion dollars of community value. Equipment may be purchased elsewhere. Specialized workers may arrive temporarily. Profits may leave the region.

The more meaningful measure is what remains after construction is finished.

That makes workforce development particularly important. Large electricity, transmission, broadband, and data infrastructure projects could support pathways into electrical work, fiber optics, networking, controls, cybersecurity, HVAC, facilities operations, and other skilled technical trades.

Rather than treating workforce programs as an ancillary community benefit, the EUP could negotiate and plan for them as part of the infrastructure itself.

### 4. Opportunity Value

The fourth dimension may be the most important for a rural region.

**Opportunity Value asks what becomes possible tomorrow because we made an infrastructure investment today.**

A transmission project, for example, should not necessarily be evaluated solely according to the electricity it delivers immediately after commissioning.

Additional capacity could enable:

- industrial expansion;
- additional renewable or firm generation;
- advanced manufacturing;
- data-center or AI infrastructure;
- electric transportation;
- building electrification;
- broadband facilities;
- research computing;
- energy storage; and
- future businesses that cannot yet be identified.

This creates an important distinction between **utilization and optionality**.

Traditional infrastructure analysis tends to reward assets that solve an identified problem. Opportunity Value recognizes that infrastructure can also expand the range of futures available to a community.

That consideration is particularly important in rural regions where infrastructure constraints can prevent opportunities from appearing in the first place.

A company cannot seriously consider locating a power-intensive operation in a community without adequate electric capacity. A telecommunications provider cannot build certain services without middle-mile connectivity. Advanced industries cannot operate without both.

The absence of infrastructure therefore has an economic effect that is difficult to see:

**the opportunities that never arrive.**

BAAJA's transmission and permitting provisions should consequently be evaluated not merely by how quickly they move existing projects through review, but by whether they enable infrastructure that increases the EUP's long-term capacity to say **yes** to future opportunities.

## A Fifth Question Across All Four Returns: Who Pays?

Holistic ROI also reveals an issue cutting across every category.

**Who pays to create the return?**

Consider a hypothetical large AI data center proposed for the EUP.

The developer might receive a strong Owner Return.

The project might generate construction employment and tax revenue, producing Community Return.

New electrical and fiber infrastructure might create Opportunity Value.

But if serving the facility requires hundreds of millions of dollars of grid investment ultimately recovered from existing electricity customers, User Return could be negative.

Conversely, if the large-load customer finances necessary transmission and distribution upgrades and some of that infrastructure creates capacity available for subsequent economic development, the project could potentially generate positive returns across **all four dimensions**.

That is the difference between simply attracting investment and **designing investment for regional benefit**.

## A Practical EUPConnect Infrastructure Test

EUPConnect should advocate evaluating significant infrastructure proposals through a common regional scorecard.

| Dimension | Core question | Example indicators |
|---|---|---|
| **Owner Return** | Can the investment operate sustainably? | Capital cost, operating cost, utilization, financing, project risk |
| **User Return** | Are users better off? | Rates, reliability, resilience, capacity, service quality |
| **Community Return** | What stays in the region? | Jobs, wages, local procurement, tax base, training, community infrastructure |
| **Opportunity Value** | What becomes possible afterward? | Available capacity, future connections, industrial sites, broadband capacity, new business potential |

Every major infrastructure proposal should be able to explain all four.

Projects need not maximize every category equally. But policymakers and communities should be able to see explicitly where value is created, who captures it, and where costs or risks are transferred.

## EUPConnect Position

EUPConnect should support the underlying goal of making essential American infrastructure easier and more predictable to build while insisting that **speed cannot be the only measure of successful permitting reform**.

The Eastern Upper Peninsula needs investment in electricity, transmission, communications, transportation, and digital infrastructure. Greater permitting predictability can help attract that investment and reduce development risk.

At the same time, reforms involving NEPA, the Clean Water Act, the Endangered Species Act, and historic preservation affect interests extending beyond project economics.[^1] In a region defined by the Great Lakes, extensive natural resources, tribal nations, rural communities, and culturally significant landscapes, environmental stewardship, tribal consultation, cultural-resource protection, and meaningful community participation must remain part of infrastructure decision-making.

Those objectives do not have to be contradictory.

**Faster decisions do not have to mean weaker decisions.**

The goal should be a system capable of making rigorous decisions more efficiently while giving developers, utilities, communities, tribal nations, regulators, and ratepayers greater certainty about both the process and the outcome.

## Recommended EUPConnect Principles

### 1. Infrastructure beneficiaries should bear an appropriate share of infrastructure costs

Large loads should not automatically transfer the costs of serving them to households and small businesses.

### 2. Host communities should participate in the return

Local employment, training, procurement, tax revenue, and infrastructure improvements should be considered during project development rather than after construction begins.

### 3. Build infrastructure with future capacity in mind

Transmission, energy, broadband, and digital infrastructure should be designed where practical to unlock additional regional development instead of serving only the first project that finances it.

### 4. Measure affordability at the household and community level

A project described as lowering system-wide costs is not enough. Policymakers should examine the actual effect on residential customers, small businesses, public institutions, and other existing users.

### 5. Evaluate the complete return

Public policy should ask not simply *how much investment will this project attract?* but:

> **What is the Owner Return? What is the User Return? What is the Community Return? And what Opportunity Value does it leave behind?**

## Conclusion: Build Faster, but Build for Return

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 represents a consequential attempt to rethink the federal processes surrounding energy and infrastructure development.[^1][^2] For the Eastern Upper Peninsula, however, its importance extends beyond permitting.

The legislation provides an opportunity to have a broader conversation about **what successful infrastructure investment actually looks like**.

The EUP should welcome infrastructure capable of strengthening the regional economy, expanding electrical and digital capacity, creating skilled careers, improving reliability, and opening doors to opportunities that do not exist today. But those outcomes cannot simply be assumed because capital has been invested.

They need to be designed into the investment.

That is the contribution Holistic ROI can make to the discussion. Instead of judging infrastructure solely by dollars invested, megawatts generated, miles constructed, permits accelerated, or jobs announced, it asks us to look at the entire system of value creation.

**Owner Return. User Return. Community Return. Opportunity Value.**

For EUPConnect, that leads to a straightforward position:

> **Build the infrastructure America needs. Build it efficiently. Protect affordability. Respect the communities and environments that make development possible. And leave behind enough capacity and opportunity that the next generation can build something we have not imagined yet.**
